## What Is Actually Happening Before Your Period

The days before menstruation are the end of the luteal phase. The corpus luteum, the structure that formed from the follicle that released your egg, is breaking down. As it does, progesterone and estrogen both fall. That hormonal drop is the main driver of the symptoms most people associate with PMS.

Understanding this mechanism is useful because it explains two things: why symptoms follow a predictable pattern (they are tied to a biological sequence), and why they vary between people (hormone levels, sensitivity, and other factors differ).

## Physical Signs Your Period Is Approaching

### Bloating and Water Retention

One of the most common premenstrual symptoms. The hormonal shift in the late luteal phase causes the body to retain water in soft tissue, which produces a feeling of fullness or puffiness, often most noticeable in the abdomen, hands, and face. It typically peaks in the day or two before bleeding begins and clears within the first couple of days of your period.

Tracking: note when bloating starts and ends, rate severity on a simple scale (mild, moderate, significant), and log whether it affects clothing or daily comfort.

### Breast Tenderness or Fullness

Rising and then falling progesterone causes breast tissue to swell and become more sensitive. The tenderness is usually bilateral (both sides) and feels diffuse rather than localized to a specific spot. It resolves once menstruation starts and progesterone drops.

Persistent, severe, or one-sided breast pain that does not follow this cycle-linked pattern warrants evaluation.

If breast tenderness is hard to explain later, use the [breast tenderness before period tracker](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker).

### Cramping Before Bleeding Starts

Some people experience cramping a day or two before their period arrives. This can be caused by the uterus beginning to contract in response to rising prostaglandin levels as the lining prepares to shed. If significant cramping occurs well before bleeding, or if it is much more intense than usual, it is worth tracking and reporting.

### Headaches and Migraines

Estrogen withdrawal, the sharp drop in estrogen near the end of the luteal phase, can trigger headaches or migraines in susceptible people. Menstrual migraines are a recognized clinical pattern, often more severe and harder to treat than non-cycle-linked migraines.

Tracking: note headache timing relative to cycle day, severity, whether it responded to your usual pain relief, and whether it accompanied or preceded the start of bleeding.

For a focused worksheet, use the [migraine before period tracker](/free/menstrual-migraine-log) or the fuller [menstrual migraine cycle log](/free/menstrual-migraine-log).

### Skin Changes

Many people notice breakouts in the premenstrual phase. Progesterone stimulates sebum production, and the hormonal environment of the late luteal phase tends to be favorable for acne. This often clears as estrogen rises in the early follicular phase.

Use the [acne before period skin log](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker) if you want to track timing, spot location, product changes, and clinician questions without choosing a treatment from a symptom list.

### Digestive Changes

Prostaglandins affect smooth muscle throughout the body, not just the uterus. This is why diarrhea, loose stools, or gut cramping at the start of a period are common. Some people also experience constipation in the luteal phase due to progesterone's slowing effect on intestinal motility.

### Fatigue and Sleep Changes

Progesterone has a sedative effect on many people. The luteal phase often brings heavier fatigue, lower motivation, and disrupted sleep, particularly the few nights before menstruation. Some people sleep more. Others report difficulty staying asleep.

## Emotional and Mood Signs

### Irritability, Low Mood, Anxiety

Mood changes in the premenstrual phase are well-documented and driven by the neurological effects of changing hormone levels on neurotransmitter systems, particularly serotonin. The severity ranges from mild irritability to significant depression or anxiety that affects daily functioning.

For most people, these symptoms resolve within the first day or two of menstruation. If they do not clear with the start of bleeding, or if they are severe enough to affect work, relationships, or daily activities, they may indicate PMDD, which is a clinical condition that responds to treatment. The [PMDD tracking guide](/resources/guides/pmdd-period-tracking-guide) covers how to document this pattern for clinical use.

### Food Cravings

Cravings, particularly for carbohydrates and sweet foods, are common in the luteal phase. Progesterone affects appetite-regulating hormones, and serotonin's role in both mood and appetite means that both tend to shift together in this phase.

For plain notes, use the [period cravings tracker](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker) or [hungry before period log](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker).

### Difficulty Concentrating

Cognitive changes in the luteal phase, often described as brain fog, slower thinking, or difficulty with complex tasks, are reported by many people. The neurological mechanisms are not fully established, but they correlate with the hormonal profile of the late luteal phase.

If focus or memory is the hard part to explain later, use the [brain fog before period tracker](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker) or [period brain fog notes](/free/premenstrual-symptom-tracker) to keep dates, daily impact, and care questions in one place.

## Using These Signs to Predict Your Period

If you track symptoms consistently alongside cycle dates, you can identify your personal premenstrual pattern. Over two to three cycles, the timing between when symptoms start and when your period arrives tends to be relatively consistent.

This is practically useful for planning. But it also builds a record that is medically useful: if your usual pattern shifts significantly, if new symptoms appear, or if your symptoms worsen, your tracking history gives you a baseline to compare against.

## When to Seek Medical Evaluation

Premenstrual symptoms that are severe enough to require prescription pain medication, cause you to miss work or cancel plans, or involve significant depression, anxiety, or suicidal thoughts are not something to manage alone or dismiss as inevitable. Effective treatments exist for both PMS and PMDD.

Also worth discussing with a provider:
- Pain that starts or worsens significantly in the premenstrual phase (can indicate endometriosis)
- Very heavy bleeding once menstruation starts
- A sudden shift in your symptom pattern without a clear explanation
- Symptoms that continue well into or past the menstrual phase

## A Note on Tracking Privacy

Premenstrual symptom logs contain sensitive information: mood records, physical health data, cycle patterns, and details that may imply reproductive status. Apps that upload data to cloud servers can create company-held copies. Flo Health settled an FTC action in 2021 over health data sharing with third parties. HHS says HIPAA applies to covered health plans, health care clearinghouses, certain health care providers, and business associates. Many consumer health apps may fall outside that system, depending on what they do and who they work with.

Apps that store core records on device can reduce company-held copies. They cannot control screenshots, exports, backups, shared phones, or records you choose to share. For breast pain, cravings, acne, anger, and mood notes, use the [PMS body data privacy checklist](/resources/privacy-in-practice/protect-your-symptom-and-condition-data) before sending a full export.
