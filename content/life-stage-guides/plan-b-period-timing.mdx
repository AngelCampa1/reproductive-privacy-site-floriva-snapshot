## What Plan B Actually Does

Plan B contains levonorgestrel, a synthetic progestin, in a higher dose than found in regular progestin-only contraceptives. Its primary mechanism of action is delaying or preventing ovulation.

The way it works depends on where in your cycle you are when you take it:

- **Before ovulation:** Plan B can delay the LH surge that triggers ovulation, preventing an egg from being released. This is when it is most effective.
- **Around the time of ovulation:** If the LH surge has already begun but ovulation has not yet occurred, Plan B may still delay it, but effectiveness decreases.
- **After ovulation:** If ovulation has already happened, Plan B cannot reverse it. The evidence does not support a reliable effect on preventing implantation at standard doses.

This means the earlier in the cycle you take Plan B (relative to your ovulation), the more effectively it can work. The sooner after unprotected sex you take it, the better the chances of it disrupting the cycle before ovulation occurs.

## How Plan B Changes Your Period

### Timing shifts

The most common experience after taking Plan B is a change in when the next period arrives. This can go in either direction:

**Earlier than expected:** If Plan B delayed ovulation, the cycle in which you took it may have a shorter-than-normal luteal phase, bringing the period sooner.

**Later than expected:** If ovulation was significantly delayed, the luteal phase occurs later, and the period follows accordingly.

**On time or close to it:** Some people's periods arrive near their expected date with minimal disruption.

All three outcomes are possible. The specific shift depends on your individual cycle, where you were in it when you took Plan B, and how your body responded to the hormonal intervention.

### Period characteristics

Beyond timing, the period following Plan B may differ in:
- **Flow:** Heavier or lighter than usual
- **Duration:** Slightly shorter or longer
- **Cramping:** May differ from your typical level

These variations are common in the first cycle after Plan B. The second cycle is usually back to your personal baseline.

### Spotting after taking Plan B

Many people notice some light spotting in the days following Plan B. This is a hormonal response to the high progestin dose and is not a period. It tends to be lighter than a period and shorter-lived. It is not a confirmation that the medication worked.

## When to Take a Pregnancy Test

If your period is more than a week late after taking Plan B, take a pregnancy test.

Emergency contraception is not 100% effective. The risk of pregnancy is reduced, not eliminated. A late period is the most important signal that pregnancy should be ruled out directly.

Signs that warrant a pregnancy test regardless of timing:
- No period within 3 to 4 weeks of when you expected it
- Unusual symptoms (breast tenderness, nausea, fatigue) that are different from your typical premenstrual pattern
- Spotting that is much lighter and shorter than your normal period in the cycle following Plan B

A negative pregnancy test taken at least a week after the expected period date is reliable with currently available tests.

## Tracking After Plan B

If you track your cycle, Plan B creates a disruption that is worth noting. Log the date you took it and the specific circumstances (where you were in your cycle if you know, the date of unprotected sex). Then track:

- Any spotting in the days following
- The date your next period arrives
- How that period compares to your typical flow
- The date your following cycle begins

With this record, you can see your cycle returning to its baseline over the next one to two cycles and have clear documentation if any irregularity persists beyond that.

## Plan B, Legal Context, and Data Privacy

In the current legal environment in some US states, emergency contraception use, period tracking data, and reproductive health records can be sensitive in ways that extend beyond personal privacy preferences. Laws and their enforcement have varied, and the legal landscape continues to change.

Period tracker apps that store your data on servers create records of your cycle dates, medication logs, and symptom entries that can be accessed by third parties. Flo Health settled FTC action in 2021 for sharing user reproductive health data with Facebook and Google. This data is not covered by HIPAA.

If you use a period tracker to log emergency contraception use or the cycle disruption that follows, an app that stores data only on your device means that record does not exist on any external server. It is less exposed to company-side requests, sharing, or breach. Device access is a separate risk.
