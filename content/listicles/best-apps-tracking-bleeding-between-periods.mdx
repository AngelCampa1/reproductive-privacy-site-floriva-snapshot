## Tracking Bleeding Between Periods: What You Actually Need

Spotting, breakthrough bleeding, and intermenstrual bleeding are not the same as a period. An app optimized for period tracking, logging start date, end date, and flow for a discrete bleed, may not handle unexpected mid-cycle bleeding well. Some apps will interpret any logged bleeding as a new cycle, which distorts predictions and obscures the pattern you're trying to document.

What you need for useful intermenstrual bleeding documentation:

**Log on any day.** The app needs to accept a bleeding entry on day 14 of your cycle without treating it as a new period start. This sounds basic but not all apps handle it cleanly.

**Flow detail.** Spotting (a few drops) is clinically different from light breakthrough bleeding, which is different from heavier intermenstrual bleeding. The distinction matters when you're building a record for a doctor.

**Symptom association.** Pain, cramping, and discharge that accompany unexpected bleeding are relevant clinical context. An app that lets you tag symptoms alongside the bleeding entry creates a more useful record.

**Export capability.** A doctor can't see your phone screen during an appointment. Formatted export, a timeline or PDF, makes the data usable in a clinical setting.

## Why This Data is Sensitive

Detailed bleeding logs are health data. [Period tracker data is not covered by HIPAA](/resources/guides/period-tracker-hipaa/). The legal protections that apply to your doctor's records do not apply to a consumer app. The FTC enforcement action against [Flo](/compare/alternatives/flo-app-alternative/) in 2021 specifically concerned sharing reproductive health data with Facebook and Google.

A multi-month log of unexpected bleeding, flow intensity, and associated symptoms is the kind of information that describes your health status in detail. Where that data lives, on a server the app company controls, or on your device, is worth thinking through before you start tracking.

## Building a Useful Clinical Record

If you're tracking intermenstrual bleeding to bring to a gynecologist, structure your logging consistently from the start: date, flow intensity (spotting/light/medium/heavy), duration, and any symptoms. Do this for every episode, not just the most dramatic ones. Three months of consistent logs is more clinically useful than an incomplete record going back two years.
