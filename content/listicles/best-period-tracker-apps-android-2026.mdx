Android is more flexible than iOS in a few ways. Apps can be distributed outside the Play Store, APK contents can be audited more easily, and Google Health Connect integration is optional rather than the built-in default.

That flexibility cuts both ways. Android's open SDK marketplace means many apps bundle advertising and analytics libraries that collect data beyond the app's stated purpose. For an app logging sensitive reproductive health data, checking what SDKs are included is a legitimate privacy step.

**What to look for in an Android period tracker:**

**Google Health Connect integration.** Google Health Connect (previously Google Fit) is the Android health data aggregator. Apps that sync to it send your menstrual data to Google's health infrastructure. Google's data use practices and response to legal requests are governed by US law. For period tracking post-Dobbs, this matters. Apps in this list that do not integrate: Floriva, Drip. Apps where it is optional: Clue. Apps that use it: Natural Cycles.

**SDK audit.** Privacy-focused apps will not include the Facebook SDK, Google Mobile Ads SDK, or major data broker analytics libraries. Floriva and Drip have been independently noted for clean SDK bundles. Flo was explicitly cited in the FTC action for sharing data via Facebook's SDK. Stardust has been verified as clean by independent reviewers.

**Notification privacy.** On Android, apps can show notification content on the lock screen. A reminder that reads "Your period is due tomorrow" is visible to anyone who sees the phone. Check app and Android system notification settings to control whether sensitive reminder text appears on the lock screen.

**Offline functionality.** Apps that require network access for core features are, by definition, sending data. Fully offline apps like Drip and Floriva, neither of which syncs, give you control over when and whether data leaves the device: only when you choose to export it yourself.
