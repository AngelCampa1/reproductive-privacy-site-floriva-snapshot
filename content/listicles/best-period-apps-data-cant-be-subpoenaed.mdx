## How We Evaluated

We evaluated each app on one question: how much readable cycle data does the company hold? Privacy policies, encryption in transit, and corporate promises matter less than the basic question of data location.

We did not weight features, prediction accuracy, or interface design heavily. When the evaluation criterion is legal protection, architecture is the only factor that matters.

## Understanding the Legal Mechanism

A subpoena is a legal order requiring a person or company to produce documents or testify. In the context of period trackers, a prosecutor investigating a suspected abortion violation can issue a subpoena to the company requesting all data associated with a specific user account, email address, or device ID.

The company can challenge the subpoena in court, but this costs money and takes time. Most app companies are not equipped or willing to fight subpoenas on behalf of individual users. Flo's privacy policy says it will challenge overbroad requests, but this is a policy commitment, not a structural guarantee.

On-device trackers eliminate this entire process. There is no data to subpoena. The company's answer to any request is short: "We don't have the data you're requesting."

## The Architecture Matters More Than the Policy

After the Dobbs decision, several period tracker companies updated their privacy policies to promise stronger data protections. Flo launched "Anonymous Mode." Clue published statements about GDPR protections. These are policy responses to a structural problem.

A privacy policy is a document that can be changed at any time, violated without users knowing (as Flo demonstrated), or overridden by legal process. On-device storage is an architectural decision that cannot be overridden by a policy change, a court order, or a corporate decision to monetize data.

We built Floriva with on-device storage because the post-Dobbs landscape requires architectural solutions. When the stakes include potential criminal prosecution, "we promise not to" is not enough.
