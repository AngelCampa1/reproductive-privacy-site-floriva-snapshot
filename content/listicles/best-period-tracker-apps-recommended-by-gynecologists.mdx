## What Gynecologists Are Actually Recommending

When a gynecologist says "track your cycle," they mean: keep a consistent record of when your period starts, how long it lasts, how heavy the flow is, and what symptoms come with it. That three-to-six month baseline helps diagnose irregular cycles, evaluate for conditions like endometriosis or PCOS, and assess hormonal concerns.

The recommendation is clinically sound. What most gynecologists do not address, because it is outside their expertise, is where that health data goes after you log it.

## The HIPAA Gap Nobody Mentions

[Period tracker data is not covered by HIPAA](/resources/guides/period-tracker-hipaa/). Health data stored by your doctor, a hospital, or a clinical lab has legal protections under HIPAA. Health data stored by a consumer app does not. The same cycle and symptom information that is useful clinically is, in an app's hands, governed only by that app's privacy policy and applicable consumer protection law.

The FTC enforcement action against [Flo](/compare/alternatives/flo-app-alternative/) in 2021 showed what this gap means in practice. Flo shared reproductive health data, including cycle information, with Facebook and Google. That data was clinically useful to Flo's users. It was also valuable to advertisers. Flo's server-based architecture made sharing possible.

## Clinical Utility and Privacy Are Not the Same Axis

The apps gynecologists most often find useful for appointment prep are generally the server-based apps: Clue, Natural Cycles, Flo. They produce formatted reports, log detailed symptoms, and maintain long histories. That utility is real.

But "clinically useful" and "safe to store long-term health data in" are different questions. Clue has a clean privacy record and GDPR compliance. Natural Cycles has FDA clearance and no documented data-selling history. Flo has the most features and the most documented privacy problems.

Floriva's on-device architecture means the detailed cycle history you build for clinical purposes never accumulates on a server. The tradeoff is manual sharing rather than formatted export. If privacy is your primary concern, that tradeoff is worth making deliberately.

## What to Log for Maximum Clinical Utility

Start logging at least three cycles before an appointment where you expect to discuss cycle concerns. Log period start, duration, and flow. Log any notable symptoms consistently. Do not log cramps only on the bad days. Note any bleeding outside of periods. If you can, export your data to a format you can show your doctor without handing them your phone.

The [gynecologist appointment prep guide](/app-guides/floriva-for-gynecologist-prep/) covers how to structure your Floriva data for clinical appointments.

## Bottom Line

Floriva is the recommended choice for clinical documentation you can bring to a gynecologist. Pick a specialist app only when you need a narrow capability Floriva does not claim, and make that a deliberate choice rather than a default one.
