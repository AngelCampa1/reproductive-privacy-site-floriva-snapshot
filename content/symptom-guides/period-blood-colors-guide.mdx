## Why Period Blood Changes Color

Menstrual fluid is not simply blood. It contains blood, shed endometrial tissue, cervical mucus, and vaginal secretions. This mix produces different appearances depending on the proportion of each component, how quickly it is flowing, and how long it has been in the body before exiting.

Color is primarily determined by oxidation. Fresh blood is bright red because hemoglobin is intact. As blood sits in the uterus or moves slowly through the vaginal canal, hemoglobin breaks down and changes color from red to brown to nearly black. Fast-flowing fresh blood exits red; slow-moving older blood exits brown or dark.

Understanding the color range that is normal for you across your cycle makes it easier to notice when something genuinely unusual appears.

This guide is for educational purposes only and is not a substitute for medical advice.

## The Full Color Spectrum

### Bright Red

Fresh, fast-flowing menstrual blood is bright red. This is most common during the heaviest days of a period, typically days 2 and 3 for most people, when the uterus is contracting actively and expelling blood quickly.

Consistently bright red blood throughout the entire period is normal for many people, particularly those with heavier flow.

### Dark Red

A deep, darker red often appears during moderate-flow days. Blood that moves at a medium pace has partially oxidized but is still relatively fresh. Dark red is well within the normal range for most of the period.

### Brown

Brown period blood indicates blood that has taken longer to exit. It has oxidized more completely. Brown blood is very common at the start of a period (old blood from the previous cycle clearing) and at the end (slow, tapering flow). Brown blood in the middle of a heavy period is less typical and can sometimes indicate that flow has slowed unexpectedly.

Brown spotting between periods, particularly mid-cycle or in the week before a period, is often old blood from a minor uterine event and is usually benign. Recurring mid-cycle brown spotting is worth documenting.

### Black or Very Dark Brown

Near-black period blood is the extreme of oxidation. It is most common at the very end of a period, in the last day or two of tapering flow. Old blood that takes a long time to exit the uterus darkens significantly. Black blood at the very end of a period is normal.

Black or very dark blood appearing at other times, or as the primary color throughout a period, can sometimes indicate that the uterus is not clearing efficiently. Worth noting if it is a new pattern.

### Pink

Pink blood is red blood diluted by cervical fluid. It appears when flow is light, most commonly at the start or end of a period. Mid-cycle pink spotting can indicate ovulation. Pink blood as the primary color across an entire period may suggest low estrogen and a thin uterine lining. Worth tracking across multiple cycles and discussing with a provider if persistent.

### Orange

Orange menstrual blood is not typical. Orange or orange-red coloring often indicates blood mixed with cervical or vaginal fluid in a way that suggests possible infection. Bacterial vaginosis and other infections can alter the appearance of vaginal discharge and blood mixing. Orange blood accompanied by unusual odor or discharge texture warrants a provider visit.

### Gray

Gray or grayish discharge, particularly if accompanied by a fishy odor, is strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis. Gray tissue-like material early in pregnancy can indicate pregnancy loss and requires medical attention. Gray period blood outside of these contexts is not typical and should be evaluated.

### Watery or Very Light Pink

Extremely watery, very lightly pink menstrual flow can suggest a diluted uterine lining and may be associated with low estrogen or significant anemia. If your flow has become progressively more watery and pale over several cycles, a hormonal evaluation is worthwhile.

## What to Log

Tracking blood color takes seconds and over time creates a useful reference:

- **Color by day**, what shade was primary on each day of the period
- **Flow rate**, was the color correlated with heavier or lighter flow
- **Odor**, normal or unusual
- **Any unusual shades**, orange, gray, or other colors outside the red-brown spectrum
- **Texture**, clots? Tissue-like material?
- **Cycle day**, where in the cycle did unusual colors appear

## When to See a Healthcare Provider

- Orange, gray, or otherwise atypical color alongside unusual odor, discharge, or pelvic symptoms
- Brown mid-cycle spotting that is new and recurs across multiple cycles
- A sustained change from your normal color pattern across several cycles
- Very light, pink, or watery flow that is new and not explained by a recent change in contraception
- Any unusual color in a confirmed pregnancy

## A Note on Logging Period Color and Privacy

Period blood color logs, like all detailed cycle data, are sensitive health information that can reveal pregnancy status, fertility windows, and reproductive health patterns.

Flo Health was subject to an FTC enforcement action in 2021 for sharing period and health data with Facebook and Google. A class action settlement followed in 2025. Period tracking data is not covered by HIPAA and can be subpoenaed from cloud-based apps in states where abortion is criminalized.

On-device period trackers like Floriva, Drip, and Euki store your color logs and cycle notes exclusively on your device. There is no cloud record to be accessed by third parties, compelled by law enforcement, or sold to advertisers.
