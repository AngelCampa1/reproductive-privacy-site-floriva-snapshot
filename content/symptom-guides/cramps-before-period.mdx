## Why Cramping Happens Before Your Period

The uterus is a muscle, and like any muscle, it contracts. Premenstrual and menstrual cramps are caused by prostaglandins, hormone-like compounds produced by the uterine lining in the late luteal phase. As the lining prepares to shed, prostaglandin levels rise, triggering contractions that squeeze blood out of the uterine vessels and eventually expel the lining itself.

These contractions begin before blood actually appears. That is why some people feel cramping for a day or two before any visible flow starts. The same process continues through the first days of the period, often peaking on day one or two of bleeding and then fading as prostaglandin levels drop and the lining has shed.

## The Normal Range for Pre-Period Cramps

There is real variation in when cramps start and how intense they are. Most people with typical, primary dysmenorrhea experience:

- Onset: 1 to 2 days before bleeding starts, sometimes up to 5 days before in some cycles
- Location: lower abdomen, often midline, sometimes radiating to the lower back or upper thighs
- Character: dull ache, pressure, or tightening; can become sharper at peak intensity
- Resolution: gradual improvement over the first 1 to 2 days of flow

Some people feel very little before the period and only experience cramping once bleeding begins. Others feel significant cramping for several days before flow. Both can be within a person's normal range if the pattern is consistent across cycles.

## When Pre-Period Cramps May Indicate Something More

### Cramps Starting Too Early

Cramping that begins a week or more before the period (especially if it is pronounced) is outside the typical primary dysmenorrhea pattern. Conditions like endometriosis or adenomyosis can cause pain throughout the cycle, including well before menstruation begins, because the displaced tissue responds to hormonal changes regardless of cycle phase.

### Pain Worsening Over Time

Primary dysmenorrhea often improves with age and after pregnancy. Pain that was manageable in earlier years but has become increasingly severe over time (requiring escalating pain relief or causing you to miss work or activities more frequently) suggests secondary dysmenorrhea and warrants investigation.

### Pain That Does Not Improve Once the Period Starts

Typical premenstrual cramps improve or resolve within the first 1 to 2 days of flow. Pain that begins before the period and persists throughout or after it is more consistent with a structural cause.

### One-Sided or Deep Pain

Cramping localized to one side, or deep pelvic pain rather than the typical frontal abdominal ache, can indicate endometriosis, ovarian cysts, or other structural issues. One-sided pain that is consistent across cycles warrants evaluation.

### Pain During Sex

Deep pain during penetrative sex alongside premenstrual or menstrual cramping is a symptom that should be evaluated, as it is a common complaint in endometriosis and adenomyosis.

## What to Track

Tracking pre-period cramps over three or more cycles creates a pattern that clinical evaluation can use:

- **Cycle day cramps begin:** count from the last period start date
- **Intensity:** use a consistent 1 to 10 scale
- **Location:** midline, one-sided, lower back, radiating
- **Character:** ache, pressure, sharp, cramping, constant, or wave-like
- **Duration:** how many days before the period? Does it continue into the period?
- **Functional impact:** did cramps cause you to cancel plans, miss work, or need prescription medication?
- **What helps:** heat, NSAIDs, rest

Six months of daily logging, even brief notes, gives a clinician far more to work with than a verbal summary.

## When to See a Healthcare Provider

Consider a provider visit if:

- Cramps begin significantly earlier than a few days before the period (more than a week)
- Cramping severity has increased over time
- Pain regularly interferes with daily life, work, or school
- You also experience pain during sex or bowel movements
- Cramps persist through the entire period and beyond
- Cramps are accompanied by heavy bleeding, unusual discharge, or fever

## A Note on Pain Data and Privacy

A detailed pain and symptom log, especially one that maps cramping patterns across cycles, constitutes sensitive health information. Pain patterns that suggest conditions like endometriosis or that map to fertility-relevant cycle events carry clinical and legal implications in certain jurisdictions.

Flo Health was subject to an FTC enforcement action in 2021 for sharing period and health data with Facebook and Google. A $59.5 million class action settled in 2025. Period tracking data is not HIPAA-protected and can be subpoenaed from cloud-based apps in states where abortion is criminalized.

On-device trackers like Floriva, Drip, and Euki store your pain logs and cycle data exclusively on your device. No readable central record exists to be compelled, sold, or breached.
