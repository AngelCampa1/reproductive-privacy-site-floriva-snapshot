Diet for estrogen dominance works on three distinct mechanisms: what the liver does with estrogen, what the gut does with processed estrogen, and what does not get into the body in the first place. Working on all three simultaneously produces the most noticeable shift.

## The Liver: Supporting Estrogen Metabolism

The liver processes estrogens in two stages. Phase I uses cytochrome P450 enzymes to convert estrogen into intermediate metabolites. Phase II conjugates these metabolites to prepare them for excretion. When Phase II is overburdened or nutrient-depleted, metabolites back up, increasing the total estrogenic load.

### Cruciferous vegetables

Broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kale, cabbage, and bok choy contain glucosinolates. These are converted to indole-3-carbinol when the plant cells are broken by chopping or chewing. Indole-3-carbinol is further converted to DIM in the stomach. Both compounds shift estrogen metabolism toward the 2-hydroxylation pathway, producing less active estrogen metabolites.

**Practical:** Aim for at least one serving of cruciferous vegetables daily. Chopping them and letting them sit for a few minutes before cooking maximizes indole-3-carbinol formation. Light steaming or sauteing is better than long boiling. Raw cruciferous vegetables are fine; the myrosinase enzyme that activates glucosinolates is heat-sensitive.

### Reduce alcohol

Alcohol impairs estrogen clearance through two mechanisms: it competes with estradiol for hepatic metabolism, and it reduces SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin), which increases free estrogen. Research shows even moderate drinking raises estrogen in premenopausal women. For someone managing estrogen dominance, reducing alcohol produces faster results than most supplements.

### Adequate protein for Phase II support

Phase II liver reactions require amino acids, particularly glycine, taurine, cysteine, and glutamine. A protein-deficient diet can impair Phase II efficiency. This does not mean high-protein dieting. It means ensuring adequate protein across the day, roughly 0.8 to 1 gram per kilogram of body weight at minimum.

### Liver-supportive foods

- **Beets:** Contain betaine, which supports liver methylation pathways
- **Dandelion greens:** Bitter compounds that stimulate bile production, and bile carries conjugated estrogens to the gut for excretion
- **Rosemary:** Contains carnosol and rosemarinic acid with Phase II-inducing properties
- **Turmeric:** Curcumin supports Phase II enzyme activity

These are supporting players, not primary interventions. Cruciferous vegetables and reduced alcohol have the strongest evidence.

## The Gut: Preventing Estrogen Reabsorption

After the liver conjugates estrogens, they travel to the gut via bile. Here, the enzyme beta-glucuronidase, produced by certain gut bacteria, can deconjugate estrogens and allow them to be reabsorbed rather than excreted. This enterohepatic recirculation increases total estrogen exposure.

### Fiber

Fiber feeds bacteria that reduce beta-glucuronidase activity and supports regular bowel movements. Slower transit means more reabsorption time. Insoluble fiber from vegetables, legumes, and whole grains is especially effective.

Practical targets: 25 to 30 grams of fiber daily. Most people eating a typical Western diet consume about half this amount. If you increase fiber rapidly, expect some digestive adjustment over one to two weeks.

### Diversity of plant foods

A gut microbiome with low beta-glucuronidase activity comes from eating a wide variety of plant foods. Different types of fiber feed different bacterial populations. Aim for 30 or more different plant foods per week, not necessarily large amounts, but variety.

## What to Reduce or Avoid

**Alcohol** is the highest-priority item.

**Refined carbohydrates and sugar** promote insulin resistance, which increases aromatase activity, converting androgens to estrogen, and contributes to higher estrogen production.

**Conventional meat and dairy** may contain exogenous hormones and hormone-disrupting compounds. Organic or grass-fed options have lower hormone residue, though the actual clinical impact for premenopausal women is debated.

**Plastics in food contact.** BPA and BPS leach into food, especially when heated. Transition food storage to glass, ceramic, or stainless steel. Never microwave food in plastic containers.

**Fragranced personal care products** often contain phthalates and parabens, which have estrogenic activity. This is a lower-impact change than diet, but worth doing over time.

## What About Soy?

Soy contains phytoestrogens (isoflavones) that bind to estrogen receptors. The conventional concern is that this increases estrogenic load. The actual clinical picture is more nuanced. Isoflavones appear to have modulatory rather than purely additive effects on estrogen receptor activity. In high-estrogen states they may competitively reduce receptor stimulation. Most evidence does not support avoiding moderate amounts of whole soy foods for people with estrogen dominance. Heavily processed soy products are a different matter and are reasonably limited.

## Diet Is the Foundation, Not the Complete Solution

These dietary changes create a better hormonal environment. For mild estrogen dominance, they may be sufficient over 2 to 3 months. For moderate or severe estrogen dominance, especially with fibroids, endometriosis, or confirmed high estrogen on blood testing, diet is one component of a broader management plan that typically includes targeted supplementation and medical evaluation.
