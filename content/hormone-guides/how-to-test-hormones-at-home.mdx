The at-home hormone testing market has grown. Some products are genuinely useful. Others are marketed more aggressively than their accuracy warrants. Here is a practical breakdown.

## LH Strips (Ovulation Predictor Kits)

**Accuracy:** High. LH urine tests are validated against serum LH and reliably detect the ovulatory surge.

**What they measure:** LH levels in urine. A positive test (control line and test line of equal or greater intensity, or a peak on a digital monitor) indicates LH has surged.

**When to test:** Start testing from cycle Day 8 to 10. Start earlier for short cycles (Day 5 to 6 for cycles under 24 days). Test once or twice daily around the expected surge window. The surge is brief and can be missed with once-daily testing.

**Best time of day:** Mid-morning to early afternoon (10 am, 2 pm) often captures surges more reliably than first morning urine. The surge typically begins overnight and peaks midday. Avoid testing immediately after large fluid intake.

**Limitations:**
- A positive LH test predicts ovulation. It does not confirm it. BBT confirmation verifies ovulation occurred.
- PCOS can cause chronic LH elevation or multiple LH peaks without ovulation, making OPKs unreliable in this group.
- Some medications (clomiphene, hCG) affect LH readings.

**Cost:** $0.10 to $1 per strip. Bulk strips are inexpensive. Connected monitors with app integration cost more but provide trend data.

## Progesterone Blood Spot Tests

**Accuracy:** Reasonable when timed correctly.

**What they measure:** Serum progesterone levels from a dried blood spot. Labs such as ZRT Laboratory process these tests.

**Critical timing requirement:** Must be drawn 7 days after confirmed ovulation. In a 28-day cycle, this is approximately Day 21. If your cycle is 32 days and you ovulate on Day 18, your test day is Day 25. An untimed progesterone draw is almost uninterpretable because progesterone ranges from near-zero (pre-ovulation) to 20 or more ng/mL (mid-luteal).

**How to confirm ovulation timing:** LH surge plus 2 days gives approximate ovulation. A BBT sustained rise confirms ovulation. Draw 6 to 7 days after the first elevated temperature. Using both gives you the most accurate timing.

**What results mean:**
- Above 10 ng/mL: adequate luteal phase progesterone for most purposes
- 15 to 25 ng/mL: optimal for implantation support
- Below 10 ng/mL on a well-timed draw: potential luteal phase defect

**Cost:** $60 to $150 depending on lab and panel.

## FSH Home Tests

**Accuracy:** Detects elevated FSH reasonably well. Does not provide quantitative results.

**What they measure:** These are lateral flow tests similar to pregnancy tests. They measure FSH in urine. They typically use a threshold of 25 to 40 mIU/mL, flagging elevated FSH that may indicate perimenopause or ovarian insufficiency.

**Timing:** Day 3 of the cycle (the conventional clinical draw day for FSH).

**Limitations:** A positive result indicates FSH is elevated, but elevated relative to what? A 28-year-old with FSH above 25 mIU/mL needs evaluation. A 47-year-old with the same value may be perimenopausal. These tests provide a flag, not a diagnosis. A quantitative serum FSH from a clinical lab provides the actual number.

## Saliva Tests

**Accuracy:** Variable and generally not recommended for clinical decision-making.

Saliva hormone levels do not reliably correlate with serum levels for estradiol and progesterone. Factors that affect saliva hormone concentrations include mouth contamination, collection time, transport conditions, and the specific form of hormone (unbound vs. protein-bound). Some practitioners use salivary cortisol testing, which has better validation, but even this has limitations.

Avoid using salivary estrogen or progesterone results as the basis for hormone treatment decisions.

## When to Go to a Lab Instead

At-home tests are useful for tracking and initial screening. Go to a clinical lab when:
- You need quantitative reference values with proper reference ranges.
- You are making treatment decisions (starting progesterone supplements, evaluating fertility, investigating PMDD).
- At-home results are ambiguous or concerning.
- You need a full hormonal panel (LH, FSH, estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, SHBG, thyroid) on the same day.

Most primary care providers and gynecologists can order these tests. Some can be ordered through direct lab services (LabCorp, Quest) without a physician referral in most US states.
