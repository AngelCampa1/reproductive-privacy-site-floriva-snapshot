Most people think their cycle data lives in one place. It usually lives in more than one.

The app has a copy. Apple Health or Health Connect may have a second copy. A screenshot sits in Photos. A refill reminder sits in a calendar. A lock screen alert says the rest out loud. A backup keeps all of it after you delete the app.

This is not a worry with no basis. The FTC alleged that Flo shared users' health data with Facebook, Google, and analytics providers after telling users it would keep that data private. Mozilla reviewed popular period and pregnancy apps and warned that many had weak privacy practices. So this checklist looks at the app and at the quiet phone settings around it.

Work through it once. Repeat it when you switch phones or try a new app.

## Before you start: who else controls this phone?

Read this part first. The steps below assume the phone is yours alone.

Mark every line that is true.

- [ ] Someone else knows the phone passcode.
- [ ] Someone else owns the Apple ID or Google account.
- [ ] The phone is in Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link.
- [ ] The phone is a school, work, or parent-owned device.
- [ ] Someone else can see lock screen alerts.
- [ ] Someone else can install, delete, or block apps.
- [ ] The phone backs up to a shared cloud account.
- [ ] You would be unsafe if someone found cycle data.

If you checked any line, treat the phone as shared. Did you check the last line? Then app hiding tools are not enough on their own.

### If someone unsafe controls the phone

Do not start by changing settings in front of them. That can create new risk.

Use a lower-risk path if you can:

- Use a phone they do not control.
- Use paper kept outside the shared space.
- Use a code in a planner if that will not put you at risk.
- Ask a local advocate to help you plan.

Has someone had unsupervised access to your phone? Then check for these:

- [ ] Screen time restrictions or monitoring software.
- [ ] A profile under Settings, General, VPN and Device Management on iOS.
- [ ] An unknown app with location and notification access.

An installed profile can provide monitoring access.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline publishes tech safety resources. So does NNEDV. Use a safe device or private browsing path first.

- National Domestic Violence Hotline: 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. Website: thehotline.org.
- NNEDV Safety Net: techsafety.org has survivor resources for safer technology use.
- Coalition Against Stalkerware: stopstalkerware.org has help detecting and removing monitoring software.

### If the phone is school, work, or parent managed

A managed device has rules a personal phone does not. Do not assume app settings cover the whole device.

Apple says a supervised device can have an organization profile. That profile controls what features the device can access. Apple says MDM can send profiles and commands. It can set device rules and check policy compliance. It can also remotely lock or wipe devices.

- [ ] Check who owns the device.
- [ ] Check who manages the account.
- [ ] On iPhone or iPad, check Settings, General, VPN & Device Management.
- [ ] Check whether app installs are logged.
- [ ] Check whether location sharing is on.
- [ ] Check whether backups go to a school or family account.

Do not remove a school profile just to hide period tracking. It may break school access or alert someone. Ask a trusted adult if you are unsure.

On Android, read your device policy. Then check settings for any school or work account or management app.

For work phones, keep it simple. Use a personal device for personal health tracking. Keep work and personal apps entirely separate.

Do you need privacy from a parent or school? Or from an employer? Then a managed device is the wrong place for period data.

## 1. Find every copy first

Start with the apps you still have.

- [ ] Open search on your phone.
- [ ] Search "period."
- [ ] Search "cycle."
- [ ] Search "ovulation."
- [ ] Search "fertility."
- [ ] Search "pregnancy."
- [ ] Search "health."
- [ ] On iPhone, open App Library and check Health and Fitness.

On Android, you can also list every app. Open Settings, tap Apps, then tap See all apps. Search or scroll for period, cycle, fertility, ovulation, pregnancy, and health apps.

Also search your launcher. Some phones hide apps in folders or app drawers.

Now find the apps you forgot. An app you deleted years ago can still have a live account.

On iPhone: App Store, tap your photo, tap Purchased, tap My Purchases, then check **Not on This iPhone**.

On Android: Play Store, tap your photo, tap Manage apps and device, tap Manage, then filter by **Not installed**.

Write down every name. For each one, ask a single question: did I make an account?

If yes, search your email for the app name. Look for sign-up mail, receipts, or privacy notices. Those emails tell you which accounts are still open.

## 2. Close accounts in the right order

Deleting the app is the last step, not the first. Uninstalling removes the app from your phone. It may not delete data the company holds.

Do it in this order:

1. Open the app.
2. Export anything you want to keep.
3. Turn off cloud sync.
4. Delete the account inside the app.
5. Confirm the deletion. Note any timeframe the app states for data removal.
6. Ask for a confirmation email. If the app sends none, email the company's privacy address.
7. Wait for the stated deletion period.
8. Uninstall.

Do not skip steps 5 through 7. Uninstalling first leaves your data on the company's servers.

Then check one more door. Go to your Google Account, open Security, and open **Your connections to third-party apps and services**. Old period or fertility apps can still be linked there. Remove access you no longer need.

Removing Google access may not delete the app's own account. Treat it as one step, not the whole cleanup.

## 3. Turn off health app access

Apple Health and Health Connect are separate stores of your cycle data. Other apps can be allowed to read them.

**iPhone.** Apple says you can review Health-compatible apps from the Health app profile screen. Look under Privacy, then Apps. You can turn read or write permission on or off by category.

- [ ] Settings, then Privacy and Security, then Health.
- [ ] Tap each app. Review what it can read. Review what it can write.
- [ ] Health app, tap your photo, tap Privacy, tap Apps. Review the same list.
- [ ] Look for cycle, period, reproductive health, symptoms, ovulation, sexual activity, and fertility.
- [ ] To clear the data itself: Health, Browse, Cycle Tracking, scroll down, Delete All Cycle Tracking Data.

**Android.** Google says Health Connect lets you review connected apps. You can turn read and write permissions on or off. Look under Permissions and data, then App permissions.

- [ ] Open Settings and search "Health Connect."
- [ ] Tap Permissions and data, then App permissions.
- [ ] Tap each app. Review menstrual cycle and reproductive health access.
- [ ] To clear data: Health Connect, then Data and access. You can delete by category, by app, or all at once.

Menu names can vary by phone. Settings may not show Health Connect. If so, open the Health Connect app if it is installed.

Health Connect is not Google Fit. If you used older apps, open Google Fit. Tap your photo, then Settings. Tap Manage connected apps. Remove what you do not use.

One caution. Turning off access changes what happens next. It does not erase data another app already copied.

## 4. Cut permissions the app does not need

Period tracking rarely needs broad access to your phone.

**iPhone.** Apple says you can review app location access. Go to Settings, Privacy and Security, then Location Services. You can turn Precise Location on or off per app.

- [ ] Settings, Privacy and Security, Location Services. Set the app to Never, or While Using. Turn Precise Location off.
- [ ] Settings, then the app name. Check Photos, Contacts, Bluetooth, and Background App Refresh.
- [ ] Settings, Privacy and Security, Tracking. Apple says this controls whether apps may track your activity across other companies' apps and websites.

**Android.** Google says you can give an app approximate location, precise location, or both.

- [ ] Settings, Location, App location permissions. Set the app to Not allowed if you do not need it.
- [ ] Use approximate location when it is enough. Turn off precise location for apps that do not need it.
- [ ] Settings, Apps, the app name, Permissions. Deny Contacts, Photos, Camera, Microphone, and Nearby devices unless you need them.
- [ ] Settings, Google, All services, Ads, Ads privacy. Review ad topics and ad measurement.
- [ ] Google says you can reset or delete the advertising ID in Android settings. Apps may still have their own settings.

Menu names can vary by phone. Search Settings for "location permission" if you need to.

If the app works without a permission, leave it off.

## 5. Fix the alerts before you hide the icon

A lock screen can say more than the app ever shows.

**iPhone.** Apple says you can choose when previews appear: Always, When Unlocked, or Never.

- [ ] Settings, Notifications, then the app.
- [ ] Set Show Previews to When Unlocked or Never.
- [ ] Turn off lock screen alerts if you share space.

**Android.** Google says you can control app notifications and what shows on the lock screen.

- [ ] Settings, Notifications, App notifications, then the app.
- [ ] Hide sensitive content on the lock screen.
- [ ] Turn off lock screen alerts if you share space.

Then check the other screens that mirror your phone: watch, tablet, laptop, and car display.

Rewrite the reminder text if the app lets you.

| Use text like this | Not text like this |
|---|---|
| Check app | Period due |
| Log today | Ovulation today |
| Health note | Fertile window |
| Quick note | Pregnancy test |

Plain reminder text can still be noticed. Use it only if it is safe.

## 6. Hide the app itself

This helps when someone may glance at your phone. It does not stop someone who has your passcode.

**iPhone.** Apple says locking or hiding an app needs Face ID, Touch ID, or a passcode. Apple also says information inside a locked app will not show up in notification previews, search, or Siri suggestions. Touch and hold the app icon, then choose the lock or hide option if your phone offers it.

**Android Private Space.** Google says Private Space opens from All Apps or Settings. It uses its own lock or the screen lock. Google says it can be hidden when locked. Open Settings, search "Private Space," set it up, move the app in, and lock it. Google also notes limits. Some activity or data can still be visible outside the space. Phone menus can vary. If you do not see Private Space, your phone or Android version may not support it.

**Samsung Secure Folder.** Samsung says Secure Folder can hide personal data and photos. It sits behind a PIN, password, pattern, or fingerprint. It has lock and auto-lock settings. Open Settings, search "Secure Folder," pick a lock, move the app and files in, and set a short auto-lock time. This is a Samsung feature, not a general Android one.

Test it once. Lock it, then try to open it.

Someone may ask why an app is locked. Use a plain script if that happens.

> I lock health apps because my phone shows too much on screen. I do it for all private apps.

Or, if you use a locked space:

> I keep private apps separate. I do it for health, money, and notes.

## 7. Photos, screenshots, and calendars

These are the copies people forget.

**Photos.** Apple says hidden photos move to the Hidden album. They do not show in Library, other albums, or widgets. Apple also says the Hidden album is locked by default on iOS 16, iPadOS 16.1, macOS Ventura, and later. Check the album on your own device to see how it behaves.

Google says Locked Folder uses your device screen lock and has a **Back up Locked Folder** setting you can turn on or off.

- [ ] Check the Hidden album or Locked Folder.
- [ ] Check Recently Deleted.
- [ ] Check the Screenshots folder for leftover copies.
- [ ] Check Downloads and your Files app.
- [ ] Check shared albums, partner albums, and family albums.
- [ ] Photos can carry time and place. Strip location before you send one.

**Calendars.** Google says you choose who to share a calendar with from Settings and sharing. Google also supports visibility choices such as default, public, and private for events and tasks where available.

- [ ] Check who your calendar is shared with.
- [ ] Rename entries that spell out period, refill, clinic, or test.
- [ ] Set sensitive entries to private if your calendar offers it.
- [ ] Check saved places and recent searches in your maps app. Remove clinic names from favorites.

Private visibility may help. It does not fix a shared account, shared device, screenshot, or alert.

## 8. Backups and shared accounts

A backup can keep app data alive after you delete the app.

Apple says iCloud Backup can include device settings, home screen layout, and app organization. It can also include app data for apps that do not already sync to iCloud.

- [ ] iPhone: Settings, your name, iCloud, iCloud Backup. Then iCloud, Apps Using iCloud.
- [ ] Android: Settings, search "Backup." Check which account backs up this phone.
- [ ] Check the app's own sync or backup setting.

Turning backup off now does not prove old copies are gone. And it does not delete data inside an app account.

Shared accounts need their own pass. Apple says Family Sharing can share Apple services and help locate devices. It can share location. It can also manage a child's device with Screen Time and parental controls.

- [ ] Does anyone else know the account password?
- [ ] Are there trusted phone numbers or recovery contacts you did not add?
- [ ] Which devices are signed in?
- [ ] Is purchase sharing or location sharing on?
- [ ] Is the phone backing up to someone else's account?

Family Sharing does not mean every person sees every health note. It does mean your account may be tied to family tools. A shared account, a shared device, and a shared backup can each create exposure.

## 9. Read the store label, then read further

Apple says developers provide App Privacy details about data they and their partners collect. Google says the Play Data safety section explains developer statements about collection, sharing, and security.

Both are disclosures written by the developer. Treat them as a first look, not proof.

On iPhone, check App Privacy. Look at Health and Fitness, Sensitive Info, and Contact Info. Then look at Identifiers, Location, Usage Data, and Data Used to Track You.

On Android, check Data safety. Look at Data collected, Data shared, Health and fitness data, Personal info, Device IDs, and Location. Check whether a delete option exists.

Then open the privacy policy and search it for "ads," "analytics," "partners," and "share."

## 10. What a VPN does and does not do

A VPN hides your traffic from your network and hides your IP from the site you reach.

It does not change what the app does after your data arrives. If a period app sends cycle data to the company's servers, that data still arrives and is still stored. A VPN also does nothing about your account, your logged entries, or your backups.

Pick the VPN with care. A VPN company can see your traffic. You are moving trust from your internet provider to that company.

Avoid free VPNs. Many make money by logging and selling your browsing data. That defeats the purpose.

Use a VPN for what it is good at. Do not treat it as a privacy fix for an app that keeps your data.

## 11. Paper is still an option

Paper does not sync, back up, or send anything anywhere. It also cannot be searched by you, and it can be found, lost, or photographed.

A common middle path is two layers. Keep dates and short scores in an app. Keep the most private notes somewhere else.

If an app says it stores data "on device," check the claim yourself. Does it require an account? Does it work with the network off? Does it offer export without sync? Does the store label list data shared with third parties? FTC guidance for health app developers points to data minimization, access limits, and clear choices. You can use that same list to judge an app.

Keep one thing in mind. HHS says the rules for a health app depend on what the app does. They also depend on what data it collects and what services it provides. Being a health app does not mean medical privacy law covers it.

## Your finished audit

You are done when you can answer all of these:

- [ ] Is this phone shared, monitored, or managed by someone else?
- [ ] Which apps hold my cycle data right now?
- [ ] Which old apps still have accounts?
- [ ] Which apps can read Apple Health or Health Connect?
- [ ] Which apps can write to them?
- [ ] Can anything private show on my lock screen?
- [ ] Where are my screenshots and photos?
- [ ] What does my calendar say out loud?
- [ ] Which backups may hold cycle data?
- [ ] Who else can reach my account?

Keep the notes. Next time this takes ten minutes instead of an hour.
