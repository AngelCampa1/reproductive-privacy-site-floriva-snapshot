## What Heavy Periods Do to Your Iron Levels

Every menstrual cycle uses up iron. The body replaces it through food, but there is a limit to how much the gut can absorb each day. When menstrual blood loss consistently exceeds what the body can replace, iron stores drop. Ferritin (stored iron) falls first. Then, once reserves run out, hemoglobin drops and anemia sets in.

This happens slowly. Heavy periods do not cause sudden anemia. They cause a gradual decline that people adapt to. You get used to feeling tired. You blame breathlessness on being out of shape. You assume lightheadedness when standing is normal. By the time many people are diagnosed, their ferritin is very low.

Tracking your bleeding is an early warning system that blood tests alone can miss. Blood tests only happen when someone orders them.

## What to Track

**Flow volume with specifics.** "Heavy" is vague. Track product type, changes per day, and saturation level. Note whether you double up (pad plus tampon), use overnight products during the day, or set alarms to change products overnight. These details tell your provider whether your blood loss is clinically significant.

**Clot size and frequency.** Clots represent concentrated blood loss. Track approximate size (dime, quarter, golf ball) and which days they appear. Persistent [large clots](/resources/symptom-guides/heavy-period-bleeding-causes) indicate the uterus is shedding faster than blood can remain liquid.

**Fatigue patterns.** Iron deficiency fatigue is different from normal cycle tiredness. It persists throughout the month, gets worse after your period when iron is most depleted, and does not improve with extra sleep. Track your daily energy on a simple scale. Note whether your worst days fall in the week after your period.

**Other iron deficiency symptoms.** Note dizziness when standing, unusual shortness of breath, a pounding heart during light activity, brittle nails, restless legs at night, and cravings for ice or non-food substances. These symptoms alongside heavy bleeding are a reason to get tested for iron deficiency.

**Period duration.** Track total days from first bleeding to last, including spotting. Bleeding beyond seven days is a flag for [menorrhagia](/resources/condition-guides/menorrhagia-heavy-periods-guide).

## How Tracking Helps With Diagnosis and Treatment

The link between heavy periods and anemia may seem obvious, but it is frequently missed in practice. Menstrual blood loss is rarely measured, and many people have normalized their bleeding over years. Your tracking data makes the pattern visible.

When you show a provider three months of data documenting hourly product changes, large clots, and eight-day periods alongside a fatigue log that keeps getting worse, the response is a blood draw, not reassurance. That is what tracking changes.

After diagnosis, tracking still matters. Rebuilding iron takes months. Monitoring whether your flow responds to treatment and whether your fatigue improves as iron levels recover gives your provider what they need to adjust the plan.

## What Your Provider Needs to See

Bring data that answers:

- How many products per day on your heaviest days, and how saturated?
- Total bleeding days per cycle
- Clot presence and size
- Fatigue severity across the month, not just during your period
- Any additional symptoms: breathlessness, dizziness, pica, restless legs
- Whether patterns have worsened over the past 6-12 months

Request a CBC (complete blood count) and ferritin level. Ferritin alone is more sensitive for early iron depletion than hemoglobin, which drops late.
