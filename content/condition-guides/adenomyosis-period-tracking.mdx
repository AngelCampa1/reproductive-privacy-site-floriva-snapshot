## What Adenomyosis Does to Your Cycle

Adenomyosis changes your period in ways that are hard to describe without data. The uterine lining grows into the muscular wall. The uterus enlarges and has trouble contracting during menstruation. This leads to heavier bleeding, longer periods, larger clots, and a deep cramping pain that builds over months and years.

Adenomyosis tends to get worse over time, not better. Cycles that felt manageable two years ago may now require backup protection and days off work. That pattern matters clinically. Without a record, it gets lost.

## What to Track

You do not need to track everything. Track the things that help a provider judge how severe your symptoms are and whether they are getting worse.

**Flow volume.** Count product changes per day and note how saturated each one was. "Changed a super tampon every 90 minutes for the first two days" tells a provider far more than "heavy flow." If you are soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row, that meets the clinical definition of [menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding)](/resources/condition-guides/menorrhagia-heavy-periods-guide) and should be reported to your provider.

**Clot size and frequency.** Clots larger than a quarter (about 2.5 cm) are clinically significant. Note the approximate size, how many per day, and which cycle days they appear. Clots from adenomyosis tend to cluster in the first two to three days.

**Pain character and timing.** Adenomyosis pain is typically a deep, widespread ache in the lower abdomen or pelvis, sometimes spreading to the lower back. Track when it starts relative to bleeding, how intense it gets, and whether over-the-counter pain relievers (like ibuprofen) help.

**Pelvic pressure and bloating.** An enlarged uterus can cause a feeling of heaviness or pressure even between periods. Noting this helps your provider tell adenomyosis apart from other causes of heavy bleeding, such as fibroids.

**Period length.** Track the total days you bleed, including any spotting before or after the main flow. Adenomyosis often pushes bleeding beyond seven days.

## How Tracking Helps With Diagnosis and Treatment

Adenomyosis is diagnosed through imaging, usually transvaginal ultrasound or MRI. But the decision to order imaging depends on what a provider sees in the clinical picture. A provider who hears "my periods are heavy" has less to work with than one who sees three months of data showing flow getting worse, clots getting bigger, and pain that no longer responds to ibuprofen.

Tracking also matters once treatment starts. If you begin hormonal therapy, a levonorgestrel IUD, or GnRH agonist treatment, your provider needs to compare your post-treatment cycles to your baseline. Without a baseline record, there is nothing to compare against except your memory, which tends to compress and distort over time.

For people considering hysterectomy, which is the definitive treatment, documented severity across multiple cycles helps support that decision both clinically and for insurance purposes.

## What Your Provider Needs to See

When you bring cycle data to an appointment, organize it around these questions:

- How many days of bleeding per cycle, and is that number increasing?
- How many product changes per day on the heaviest days?
- Are clots present, and what size?
- What is the pain level on a 0-10 scale, and does it respond to over-the-counter medication?
- Are there symptoms between periods (pressure, bloating, pain during sex)?

Three cycles is the minimum. It gives your provider enough data to see patterns instead of single data points. Six cycles is better for tracking whether things are getting worse.
