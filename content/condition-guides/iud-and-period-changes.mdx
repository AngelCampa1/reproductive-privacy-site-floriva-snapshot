## Why IUDs Change Your Period

The IUD sits inside the uterine cavity, and its presence, whether hormonal or copper, directly affects the environment in which the uterine lining grows and sheds. Understanding the mechanism behind the change helps you interpret what you experience after insertion.

This guide is for educational purposes only and is not a substitute for medical advice.

## Hormonal IUDs: What Changes and Why

Hormonal IUDs release levonorgestrel, a synthetic progestin, locally into the uterine cavity at a low, steady rate. The concentration in the uterus is high, while systemic exposure is comparatively low. This local progestin does several things:

It thins the endometrium, the uterine lining. A thinner lining sheds less, and for many users it eventually becomes too thin to produce visible bleeding at all. This is amenorrhea, and it is the expected outcome for a significant proportion of people using higher-dose hormonal IUDs after about a year.

It thickens cervical mucus, which is the primary contraceptive mechanism for preventing fertilization.

It may partially suppress ovulation in some users, though this is not consistent or complete.

The result in the months after insertion is typically a period that becomes lighter with each cycle, interspersed with unpredictable spotting. The spotting tends to improve as the body adjusts to the device.

### What the first six months often look like

- Irregular spotting on unpredictable days
- Periods that are shorter or lighter than before
- Occasional cramping that may not align with the usual cycle timing
- Gradual reduction in both flow and predictability

This is expected. The frustrating part is that "irregular" means different things for different people, and the adjustment period can feel prolonged if you are used to predictable cycles.

## Copper IUDs: What Changes and Why

Copper IUDs work without hormones. Copper ions released into the uterine cavity are toxic to sperm, preventing fertilization. The device does not thin the lining, suppress ovulation, or alter the hormonal cycle.

What it does do, for many users, is affect prostaglandin production. The uterus responds to the copper as a foreign body by increasing local prostaglandin release. Prostaglandins drive uterine contractions. More prostaglandins means more intense contractions and a heavier, more painful period.

Heavier flow with a copper IUD also has a physical component: the device occupies space in the cavity and can interfere with the normal mechanism of the lining shedding in a coordinated way, sometimes producing more substantial bleeding.

### What the first several months often look like

- Heavier flow than before insertion
- More painful cramping
- Longer periods
- Some gradual improvement, though many users report ongoing heavier periods throughout

NSAIDs taken before flow begins can reduce cramping somewhat because they block prostaglandin production. This is worth trying if copper IUD cramping is a significant issue.

## What to Track After Insertion

Tracking after IUD insertion serves two purposes: it documents your personal adjustment timeline, and it provides a baseline for identifying something that has changed unexpectedly.

### Log every bleed or spot

Note the date, duration, and flow level. With a hormonal IUD, this means logging even very light spotting, because you are watching for the overall trend toward less frequent and lighter events. With a copper IUD, you are tracking whether your flow is gradually stabilizing.

### Rate your pain

Insertion and the first few days after are often painful. Track daily pain in the weeks following insertion, then continue logging pain with each period. This tells you whether cramping is decreasing toward a new normal or persisting at an elevated level.

### Note any symptoms of concern

Fever, unusual vaginal discharge, or pain that is severe and worsening rather than stable should be logged and evaluated promptly. These can indicate insertion-related complications including infection, which is uncommon but requires rapid treatment.

### Check your strings periodically

Although this is not strictly a cycle-tracking activity, noting when you last confirmed the IUD strings are in place is worth keeping in your log. If you cannot feel them when you expect to, that is a reason to contact your provider.

## A Word on Privacy

Your contraceptive method, cycle patterns during and after IUD use, and reproductive decisions generally are sensitive information. In some states, this data could carry legal implications. Apps that store period and contraceptive data on company servers can be compelled to produce that information in legal proceedings, and several major femtech companies have shared health data with advertisers without user awareness.

Tracking your IUD adjustment on a device that stores data locally, rather than uploading it to a company's servers, means that information stays with you.
