## What Fibroids Do to Your Cycle

Uterine fibroids are common. Research suggests they affect a significant proportion of people with a uterus by age 50, with higher prevalence in Black women. Most fibroids cause no symptoms. But when they do affect your cycle, the changes can be significant and disruptive.

The impact depends mainly on where the fibroid sits. Submucosal fibroids (which grow into the uterine cavity) are most likely to cause heavy bleeding. Intramural fibroids (within the muscular wall) can cause bleeding when large enough to distort the cavity. Subserosal fibroids (on the outer surface) rarely affect bleeding but can cause pressure symptoms.

Common cycle changes from fibroids include heavier flow, longer bleeding, more clotting, shorter cycle length, and spotting between periods. These changes tend to develop gradually, which makes it easy to normalize them until they become severe.

## What to Track

**Flow volume and duration.** Track daily product changes and saturation level. Note whether your heaviest day has shifted, or whether you now have multiple heavy days where you previously had one. If bleeding goes beyond seven days, record the total. Compare month to month to spot any progression.

**Clot size and timing.** [Clots](/resources/symptom-guides/heavy-period-bleeding-causes) larger than a quarter are clinically notable. With fibroids, clots may be larger and more frequent than with other causes of heavy bleeding. Record approximate size and which days they appear.

**Cycle length changes.** Fibroids can shorten cycle length by changing how the uterine lining sheds. If your cycles were 28 to 30 days and are now consistently 24 to 25 days, that shift is worth documenting.

**Bleeding between periods.** Spotting or light bleeding between periods can indicate a submucosal fibroid. Track any non-period bleeding with dates and volume.

**Pelvic pressure and urinary symptoms.** Large fibroids can press on the bladder (causing frequency or urgency) or the bowel (causing constipation). These symptoms are relevant to treatment decisions even though they are not directly about your period.

## How Tracking Helps With Diagnosis and Treatment

Fibroids are diagnosed by imaging, usually pelvic ultrasound. But the decision to treat depends on symptom severity, not just fibroid size. A 4 cm fibroid causing [heavy menstrual bleeding](/resources/condition-guides/menorrhagia-heavy-periods-guide) that requires iron supplementation is treated differently from a 6 cm fibroid causing no symptoms.

Your tracking data tells the clinical story: how much blood you are losing, whether symptoms are getting worse, and how your cycle responds to treatment. This matters especially for decisions between watchful waiting, hormonal management, or procedures such as uterine artery embolization, myomectomy, or hysterectomy.

After treatment, tracking lets you and your provider measure the response. Did flow decrease? Did cycle length return to normal? Did clotting resolve? Those comparisons require a baseline.

## What Your Provider Needs to See

Bring cycle data organized around severity and progression:

- Average and peak daily product changes on heavy days
- Total bleeding days per cycle
- Clot frequency and size
- Any between-period bleeding
- Non-menstrual symptoms (pressure, urinary changes, pain during sex)
- Whether symptoms are stable, gradually worsening, or suddenly changed

Three to six cycles of data gives the clearest picture for treatment planning.
