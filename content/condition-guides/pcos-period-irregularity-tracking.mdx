    definition: "A hormonal condition affecting people with ovaries, characterized by elevated androgens, disrupted ovulation, and often the presence of multiple small follicles on the ovaries. It is one of the most common endocrine conditions in reproductive-age people. Diagnosis requires meeting at least two of three Rotterdam criteria: irregular ovulation, elevated androgens, and characteristic ovarian appearance on ultrasound."
  - term: "Androgen excess"
    definition: "Higher-than-typical levels of androgens (including testosterone and DHEA-S) in people with ovaries. In PCOS, excess androgens disrupt follicle development in the ovaries, impair ovulation, and produce symptoms including acne, excess hair growth (hirsutism), and sometimes scalp hair thinning."
  - term: "Follicular arrest"
    definition: "The failure of ovarian follicles to mature and release an egg. In PCOS, multiple follicles begin developing but stall at a small size rather than one dominant follicle continuing to grow and ovulate. The arrested follicles are the 'cysts' visible on ultrasound (though they are follicles, not true cysts)."
  - term: "Anovulatory bleeding"
    definition: "Uterine bleeding that occurs without a preceding ovulation. In PCOS, prolonged exposure to estrogen without the progesterone rise that follows ovulation causes the uterine lining to build up and then shed irregularly or all at once. This bleeding differs from a true period in its pattern and may be heavier or more unpredictable."
  - term: "Insulin resistance"
    definition: "A condition in which cells do not respond efficiently to insulin. Insulin resistance is common in PCOS and contributes to elevated insulin levels, which in turn stimulate androgen production by the ovaries. Managing insulin resistance through diet, exercise, and sometimes medication can improve PCOS symptoms including cycle irregularity."

## What PCOS Actually Does to Your Cycle

To understand why PCOS disrupts periods, it helps to know what a regular cycle requires. Each cycle involves a coordinated hormonal sequence. FSH from the pituitary stimulates follicle development. One follicle becomes dominant and releases an egg. LH surges to trigger that release. The remaining corpus luteum produces progesterone, which stabilizes the uterine lining. When progesterone declines, the period begins.

PCOS interferes at the follicle development stage. Elevated androgens, often amplified by elevated insulin, prevent any single follicle from becoming dominant. Multiple follicles begin to grow and then stall. Without a dominant follicle, there is no ovulation, no corpus luteum, and no progesterone surge and decline. The cycle does not complete normally.

## What Your Bleeding Pattern May Look Like

Because PCOS disrupts ovulation rather than menstruation itself, the bleeding pattern is variable and depends on what happens to the uterine lining in the absence of regular progesterone cycling.

### Long cycles and infrequent periods

Without ovulation triggering a predictable progesterone decline, the lining may build up for weeks or months before shedding. This produces cycles that are 40, 60, or 90 days long, or longer. When the period does arrive after a long stretch, it may be heavy and prolonged because more lining has accumulated.

### Anovulatory spotting

Some people with PCOS experience irregular spotting or light bleeding rather than true periods. This can occur when estrogen levels fluctuate and the lining sheds incompletely without the organized progesterone-driven mechanism of a true menstrual cycle.

### Occasional regular cycles

PCOS is not an all-or-nothing condition. Some cycles, even in people with PCOS, include ovulation. You might have a 35-day cycle followed by a 90-day one followed by a 28-day one. This variability is itself characteristic.

## Androgen Symptoms to Log Alongside Cycle Data

PCOS is a hormonal condition, not just a cycle condition. Androgen-related symptoms fluctuate and often correlate with cycle patterns. Tracking them gives a more complete picture.

### Acne

PCOS-related acne is typically hormonal in distribution: jaw, chin, lower cheeks, and sometimes the back and chest. It often worsens in the late cycle when progesterone (if ovulation occurred) or androgen effects are dominant. Log location and severity weekly.

### Hirsutism

Excess hair growth on the face, neck, chest, abdomen, or inner thighs is driven by androgens. This is a slow-changing symptom that is easier to assess monthly than daily, but noting any perceived changes over time builds a meaningful record.

### Scalp hair thinning

Androgenic alopecia can affect the crown and temples in people with PCOS. Monthly photos under the same lighting conditions are more useful than daily observation for tracking slow changes.

### Energy and mood

Both are affected by hormonal fluctuations and insulin resistance. Many people with PCOS report fatigue, brain fog, and mood variability that does not correspond to a typical premenstrual pattern. Logging these daily reveals whether they correlate with any identifiable cycle phase.

## Tracking for Treatment Monitoring

If you are on treatment for PCOS, your tracking data is how you know whether it is working. Treatment may include hormonal contraception to regulate cycles, metformin or inositol for insulin resistance, or lifestyle changes.

Before you begin or change treatment, document your baseline: current cycle length range, how often you are getting periods, and what your acne and energy patterns look like. Continue tracking after the change and look for differences after three to six months. Memory is unreliable for this. Logs are not.

### Tracking ovulation with BBT

Basal body temperature, measured first thing in the morning before getting up, rises slightly after ovulation due to progesterone. A sustained temperature rise of about 0.2°C (0.4°F) lasting at least 10 days indicates that ovulation likely occurred in that cycle. In PCOS, many cycles will show a flat or chaotic temperature pattern without a clear shift, indicating anovulation. Cycles that do show a shift are evidence of ovulation. Over several months, this data shows whether ovulatory cycles are becoming more frequent.

## Privacy and PCOS Data

PCOS is a medical condition with implications for insurance coverage, fertility treatment, and in some cases employment. Data about irregular cycles, androgen symptoms, and reproductive concerns is sensitive health information.

Period tracking apps that upload data to company servers create records that can be breached, shared with advertisers, or produced under legal compulsion. Flo Health settled an FTC enforcement action in 2021 for sharing user data, including menstrual cycle information, with Facebook and Google for targeted advertising. A class action settlement followed in 2025.
