## What Vulvodynia Looks Like Across a Cycle

Vulvodynia is a diagnosis of exclusion. It is chronic vulvar pain that persists after infections, skin conditions, and other identifiable causes have been ruled out. Exact prevalence is debated because many cases go undiagnosed or misdiagnosed.

Vulvodynia is particularly difficult to manage because of its variability. Pain may be constant for weeks, then ease. It may spike before your period and settle after. It may worsen with stress, improve on certain medications, and return without clear explanation.

For people who menstruate, that variability often has a pattern. But the pattern is only visible with consistent tracking across multiple cycles. A single appointment snapshot captures one day. A three-month symptom log captures the rhythm.

## What to Track

**Pain location.** Vulvodynia can be generalized (affecting the entire vulva) or localized (typically the vestibule, the tissue around the vaginal opening). Map where you feel pain each day. Use simple descriptors: vestibule, labia, clitoris, perineum. If pain shifts locations across your cycle, that pattern is clinically interesting.

**Pain intensity and character.** Rate pain daily on a 0-10 scale. Note the quality: burning, stinging, raw, aching, throbbing. Different qualities may respond to different treatments. Burning and stinging may suggest nerve involvement. Aching may suggest muscle tension.

**Provoked vs. unprovoked.** Record whether pain occurred spontaneously or was triggered. Common triggers include tampon insertion, sexual contact, prolonged sitting, tight clothing, and exercise. If provoked pain worsens in certain cycle phases, that suggests hormonal tissue sensitivity.

**Cycle day correlation.** Mark each entry with your cycle day. After two to three cycles, look for patterns. Does pain peak in the luteal phase? Around ovulation? During menstruation? This correlation is what your provider needs.

**Treatments and their effect.** Log any topical treatments, medications, physical therapy sessions, or behavioral changes alongside your pain data. This creates a treatment response record that prevents repeating ineffective approaches.

## How Tracking Helps With Diagnosis and Treatment

Vulvodynia is managed rather than cured, and management depends on understanding the individual's pattern. A provider working with someone whose pain is worst in the [late luteal phase](/resources/symptom-guides/luteal-phase-symptoms) may consider different hormonal approaches than one working with someone whose pain is constant and cycle-independent.

Tracking also prevents the diagnostic treadmill. Many people with vulvodynia see multiple providers before getting a diagnosis. Each new provider asks the same questions. A symptom log with cycle correlation, trigger mapping, and treatment history gives each new provider the full picture without starting from zero.

For people using multimodal treatment (topical estrogen, pelvic floor physical therapy, nerve blocks, cognitive behavioral therapy), tracking which interventions correlate with improvement helps refine the plan over time.

## What Your Provider Needs to See

A useful vulvodynia tracking summary includes:

- Daily or near-daily pain ratings with cycle day marked
- Provoked vs. unprovoked distinction
- Identified triggers (specific activities, products, positions)
- Any cyclic pattern visible over two or more cycles
- Current and past treatments with their effects
- Impact on daily function (work, exercise, relationships)

Providers who specialize in vulvodynia expect this level of detail. It is the foundation of individualized treatment.
