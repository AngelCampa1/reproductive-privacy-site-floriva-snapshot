## What Fibroids Are and Who Gets Them

Uterine fibroids (also called leiomyomas or myomas) are the most common noncancerous growths of the uterus. They develop from the smooth muscle cells of the uterine wall and can grow as a single fibroid or in clusters. Their prevalence is high. The majority of people with a uterus will develop at least one fibroid at some point during their lifetime, though many never know it because symptoms are absent.

They are not cancer and do not become cancer. The risk of a uterine sarcoma, a cancerous uterine tumor, is very low and is distinct from fibroid disease.

This guide is for educational purposes only and is not a substitute for medical advice.

## How Fibroids Affect the Menstrual Cycle

### Heavy Bleeding

The most disruptive symptom for most people is menorrhagia, heavy menstrual flow. The mechanism varies by fibroid type:

- **Submucosal fibroids** increase the surface area of the uterine lining, producing more tissue to shed and interfering with the normal muscular contractions that limit bleeding. They also compress the blood vessels that would otherwise restrict flow.
- **Intramural fibroids** can prevent the uterus from contracting efficiently, allowing more blood to escape during menstruation.
- **Subserosal fibroids** rarely affect bleeding volume significantly.

Heavy periods from fibroids typically worsen over time rather than fluctuating. What begins as a heavier-than-usual period can progress to soaking through pads and tampons in an hour, passing large clots, and experiencing cycle-related anemia (fatigue, dizziness, pallor).

### Longer Periods

Fibroids can extend the duration of menstruation beyond the person's previous baseline. A period that was five days may become seven or eight. Combined with heavier flow, this substantially increases total blood loss per cycle.

### Cycle Timing Changes

Submucosal fibroids in particular can cause breakthrough bleeding or spotting outside of the expected period. Larger fibroids may alter uterine tone and hormone metabolism in ways that affect cycle regularity.

### Pelvic Symptoms

Depending on location and size, fibroids can cause:

- Chronic pelvic pressure or a sensation of heaviness in the pelvis
- Frequent urination or difficulty fully emptying the bladder
- Constipation or pressure on the rectum
- Lower back pain or pain that radiates to the legs
- Abdominal bloating significant enough to affect clothing fit or be visible

Painful periods (dysmenorrhea) are common but not universal. Some people with significant fibroids have surprisingly little pain.

## What to Track

Fibroid-related symptoms are among the most useful to document carefully because the severity of symptoms, not simply the presence of fibroids, drives treatment decisions.

### Menstrual Flow

The most objective way to track heavy bleeding is to log:

- **Products used per day**: how many pads, tampons, cups, or discs, and whether they are regular or super absorbency
- **Saturation at change**: was the product fully soaked or partially used
- **Clots**: presence, approximate size (small/large, under or over a quarter)
- **Days of heavy flow vs. moderate vs. light**

This gives a healthcare provider an actual estimate of blood loss rather than a vague description.

### Period Duration

Log the start and end date of each period. If you define "end" as the last day of any flow including spotting, use that consistently.

### Pelvic Symptoms

Rate daily pelvic discomfort on a consistent scale (0 to 10). Note location, type (pressure vs. cramping vs. sharp), and anything that makes it better or worse. Log bladder and bowel symptoms separately.

### Functional Impact

Fibroids affect daily life in ways that matter clinically. Note days when you had to change plans because of bleeding or pain, missed work or other obligations, or avoided activities. Functional impact data is used in treatment conversations and can support accommodation requests.

## Privacy and Fibroid Data

Fibroid-related tracking includes menstrual flow details, pelvic pain records, and potentially fertility-related data, all of which constitute sensitive health information. Period tracking apps that store data in the cloud can share this with advertisers or produce it in response to legal compulsion.

Flo Health settled a 2021 FTC enforcement action for sharing user period and health data with Facebook and Google, despite promising users their data was private. A $59.5 million class action settlement followed in 2025. Period tracking data is not covered by HIPAA and can be subpoenaed in any state.

An on-device tracker like Floriva keeps your logs on your phone exclusively. The company cannot access them, and they cannot be obtained remotely by third parties.

## When to See a Healthcare Provider

See a healthcare provider if you experience:

- Periods that are heavy enough to require changing protection every one to two hours
- Periods that last longer than seven days
- Clots larger than a quarter during menstruation
- Pelvic pressure, bloating, or pain that is interfering with daily activities
- Fatigue or dizziness that may indicate anemia from blood loss
- Bladder or bowel symptoms associated with cycle changes
- Unexpected bleeding outside of your expected period
- Difficulty conceiving if you know or suspect you have fibroids

A pelvic ultrasound is the standard first step in evaluation. Bring several months of bleeding logs to the appointment. The more specific your records, the more useful the visit will be.
