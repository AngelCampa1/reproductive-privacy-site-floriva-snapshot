The fertile window is narrower than most people think, and its timing is less predictable than period apps suggest. The common advice to "try on Day 14" is based on a population average that is wrong for most individual cycles, sometimes by a week or more.

## The Biology of the Fertile Window

Two biological facts define the fertile window.

1. **Egg viability:** After ovulation, the egg survives for 12 to 24 hours. If no sperm reaches it in that time, the cycle ends without fertilization.

2. **Sperm viability:** In fertile cervical mucus (the thin, slippery, egg-white consistency produced when estrogen is high), sperm can survive for up to 5 days. Without this mucus, or in the thick, hostile mucus that fills the rest of the cycle, sperm die within hours.

Together, these two facts define the fertile window: the 5 days before ovulation (when sperm deposited in fertile mucus can survive to meet the egg when it is released) plus ovulation day itself (the last day the egg is viable).

That is 6 days total. Most pregnancies result from sex 1 to 2 days before ovulation, when sperm are already in the fallopian tube waiting.

## Why "Day 14" Is Wrong for Most People

The Day 14 assumption comes from the 1930s Ogino-Knaus rhythm method, which based calendar predictions on average cycle length data. The logic was: 28-day cycle, ovulation on Day 14.

The problem is that even people with 28-day average cycles do not consistently ovulate on Day 14. Cycle length variation leads to ovulation variation. Someone with cycles ranging from 26 to 32 days could ovulate anywhere from around Day 12 to Day 18, a 6-day range.

**What population data shows:**

A 2000 study by Wilcox and colleagues published in the New England Journal of Medicine tracked daily urinary hormone levels in 221 women and found:
- Only 30% of women had their fertile window fall entirely within Days 10 to 17, the range most standard advice covers
- Fertile windows fell outside this range even in women with regular 28-day cycles
- Fertile windows were found on every day of the cycle from Day 6 to Day 21

A 2003 follow-up found that for women with irregular cycles, the variation was even greater, and could only be predicted with direct ovulation monitoring.

Day 14 is not useless. It is a reasonable starting point for someone with no data. But it is not a reliable endpoint.

## Tracking the Fertile Window: Three Approaches

### LH Test Strips

LH (luteinizing hormone) surges 24 to 36 hours before ovulation. An LH test strip detects this surge in urine, giving you a 1 to 2 day heads-up.

**How to use them:**
- Start testing from Day 8 to 10 of your cycle (earlier for shorter cycles, or if you have PCOS, which can produce multiple LH surges)
- Test at the same time each day, ideally late morning to early afternoon (LH tends to peak mid-morning)
- Test twice daily if you can, once around 10am and once around 2 to 6pm, to avoid missing a short surge
- A positive test (test line as dark as or darker than the control line on standard strips) means ovulation is likely in the next 24 to 36 hours

A positive LH test means the fertile window is open now. The peak day (the last day of peak fertility signs) lines up closely with ovulation day.

### Cervical Mucus

Cervical mucus changes under estrogen's influence in the days before ovulation:
- After your period: dry or sticky, minimal
- Rising estrogen: creamy, white or yellowish
- Peak fertility: clear, slippery, stretchy (sometimes called egg-white consistency)
- After ovulation: thickens quickly under progesterone, then dries out

The fertile window in cervical mucus terms spans from the first day of any wet or slippery sensation through the last day of egg-white mucus (the peak day). The days before peak mucus are fertile because sperm can survive in this environment. The day after peak mucus, fertility drops fast.

Cervical mucus gives you more days of information than LH strips alone, because it captures the full fertile window, not just the 24 to 36 hours before ovulation. Using both together, mucus for the leading days and LH strips for confirmation, gives the most complete picture.

### BBT Charting

Basal body temperature rises 0.2 to 0.5°C after ovulation due to progesterone. This rise confirms that ovulation happened.

The limitation is that you cannot act on it in the current cycle. By the time the temperature rise is clear, the fertile window has already closed. BBT is most useful for:
- Confirming that you are actually ovulating (and not having anovulatory cycles)
- Identifying your typical luteal phase length (from temperature rise to period)
- Building a 3-cycle or longer pattern that improves your LH strip start day and helps set expectations

If BBT consistently shows a two-phase pattern, you are ovulating. If it stays flat (monophasic), you may be having anovulatory cycles. That means no fertile window at all, regardless of what the calendar shows.

## The Fertile Window and Cycle Variation

Even with tracking, the fertile window shifts. A stressful week, a short illness, travel, or severe sleep disruption can delay ovulation by several days or more. Your period follows, usually 10 to 16 days later.

If you are trying to conceive and timing sex around a fixed calendar day, you may consistently miss the window in the cycles where ovulation was delayed. People most likely to time correctly during stressful or disrupted cycles are those tracking in real time, not those relying on averages.

**If you are trying to avoid pregnancy:** the same principle applies in the other direction. An unexpected delay in ovulation can move the fertile window into what was previously a "safe" part of the cycle. Calendar-only methods are not reliable contraception for this reason.

## Fertile Window vs. Cycle Day: A Practical Example

Consider two cycles from the same person:

| Cycle | Cycle Length | Ovulation Day | Fertile Window |
|-------|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Cycle A | 28 days | Day 14 | Days 9-14 |
| Cycle B | 32 days | Day 18 | Days 13-18 |
| Cycle C | 26 days | Day 12 | Days 7-12 |

In this example, Day 14 intercourse would fall within the fertile window in Cycle A, miss it entirely in Cycle C, and fall in the late fertile window in Cycle B. The three cycles look "regular" from the outside (the periods arrive roughly monthly) but the fertile window moves by nearly two weeks across them.

This is not unusual. It is typical cycle variation.

## What This Means for Floriva Users

Tracking period start dates alone cannot identify the fertile window. It can estimate average cycle length and suggest roughly when ovulation might occur, but for anything more precise, ovulation tracking is needed. Logging LH test results and cervical mucus observations around expected ovulation gives you your actual fertile window, not a model's estimate. Across three or more cycles, this data also reveals your personal ovulation timing pattern, which is more useful than any population average.
