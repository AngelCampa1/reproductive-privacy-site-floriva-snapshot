Flo improved its privacy practices after the 2021 FTC enforcement action, but its core architecture hasn't changed. Your data is still stored on Flo's servers, which can be subpoenaed.

## What Flo Changed After the FTC Action

The FTC consent order required Flo to take specific corrective steps. Flo:

- Notified affected users that their health data had been shared with Facebook, Google, and Flurry
- Directed those companies to delete the improperly shared data
- Updated its privacy policy
- Launched Anonymous Mode, which claims to decouple cycle data from user identity
- Implemented a privacy program as required by the consent order

These are real changes. They address the specific conduct the FTC found deceptive, sharing data with third-party SDKs while claiming not to.

Source: [FTC consent order, June 2021](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google)

## What Didn't Change

The data architecture. Flo is still a cloud-based app. Your cycle data, period dates, symptoms, pregnancy status, is stored on Flo's servers. That has not changed.

This matters because:

**Servers can be subpoenaed.** Any data a company holds on its servers can be obtained through legal process. In states with abortion restrictions, law enforcement has sought digital health data as evidence. The FTC's 2024 research paper found that "period tracking apps' data practices can be used to detect pregnancy and abortion, hence putting women at risk of being prosecuted."

Source: [FTC research paper, 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/10-Laabadli-Understanding-Womens-Privacy-Concerns-Toward-Period-Tracking-Apps-in-the-Post-Roe-v-Wade-Era.pdf)

**Policy controls can fail.** The original FTC action proves this point. Flo had a privacy policy that said it wouldn't share data. It shared data anyway. Policy controls depend on a company keeping its promises. The consent order adds oversight, but oversight is not infallibility.

**A $59.5M settlement happened despite the privacy policy.** The combined settlement across Flurry ($3.5M), Google+Flo ($56M), and the Meta jury verdict resolved claims from conduct that Flo's own policy prohibited.

Source: [Reuters, September 2025](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/boards-policy-regulation/google-flo-health-pay-56-million-period-tracking-app-privacy-case-2025-09-25/)

## Anonymous Mode Limitations

Flo's Anonymous Mode is the company's primary post-FTC privacy feature. Two limitations:

**It requires a paid subscription.** Anonymous Mode is only available to Flo Premium subscribers. Free-tier users track their cycles without the identity-decoupling that Anonymous Mode claims to provide. Basic privacy is a paid feature.

**It doesn't change the architecture.** Anonymous Mode claims to remove identifying information from cycle data within Flo's systems. But the data is still on Flo's servers. Information security researchers have warned that features like Anonymous Mode may give users a false sense of security without changing the underlying risk.

Source: [JHU Information Security Institute, July 2022](https://isi.jhu.edu/2022/07/19/deleting-your-period-tracker-wont-keep-your-health-data-private/)

## Post-Roe Implications

The Dobbs decision overturning Roe v. Wade in 2022 changed the risk calculation for period tracker data. In states with abortion restrictions, cycle data showing a missed period followed by a return to regular cycles could be interpreted as evidence of a terminated pregnancy.

NPR reported in 2022 that privacy experts warned period app data "could potentially be used to penalize anyone" in a post-Roe environment. Forbes reported in 2024 that "femtech apps that share data with third parties can put your reproductive privacy at risk."

Source: [NPR, May 2022](https://www.npr.org/2022/05/10/1097482967/roe-v-wade-supreme-court-abortion-period-apps); [Forbes, November 2024](https://www.forbes.com/sites/abigaildubiniecki/2024/11/14/post-roe-your-period-app-data-could-be-used-against-you/)

The University of Maryland Law Journal's 2025 analysis "examines the legal and policy implications of law enforcement's access to data collected by period tracking apps."

Source: [University of Maryland Law Journal, 2025](https://digitalcommons.law.umaryland.edu/jhclp/vol28/iss2/2/)

## How On-Device Alternatives Differ

The difference between Flo and on-device trackers is architectural, not just policy.

On-device trackers store all data locally on your phone. No company server holds a copy. There is no database to breach, no server to subpoena, no employee who can be compelled to produce records.

On-device storage removes the structural possibility of data exposure. No trust required.

Floriva stores all cycle data on your device using encrypted local storage. There is no sync, so nothing is transmitted. If you want your history on a new phone, you export an encrypted backup file yourself. Floriva has no server that holds decryptable reproductive health data.

Other on-device options include Euki (free, nonprofit) and Drip (free, open source, Android only). Consumer Reports recommended Drip, Euki, and Periodical as apps that "store data locally and don't allow third-party tracking."

Source: [Consumer Reports, August 2022](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/health-privacy/period-tracker-apps-privacy-a2278134145/)
