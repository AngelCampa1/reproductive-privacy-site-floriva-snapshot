## Two apps, different privacy claims

[Stardust](/compare/alternatives/stardust-alternative-local-storage/) and Clue both position themselves as safer choices for period tracking. Stardust uses [zero-knowledge](/resources/guides/what-is-zero-knowledge-period-tracker/) marketing language and an astrology-tied differentiator. [Clue](/compare/alternatives/clue-alternative-no-cloud/) uses GDPR jurisdiction, a subscription business model, and academic research partnerships.

We compared the two because users considering them are usually trying to move off Flo or another data-selling app. They want a tracker with a credible privacy story. The question is whether either app delivers privacy at the architecture level, or only at the marketing level.

## How Stardust stores data

Stardust is a US-based app marketed as privacy-first. Its public-facing copy invokes zero-knowledge encryption. When researchers examined the actual storage model, they found that user data sits on Stardust servers in a form the company can decrypt. That is not what zero-knowledge means.

True zero-knowledge means the encryption keys never leave the user's device, so even the service provider cannot read the stored data. Stardust's architecture does not match that standard, based on the reviews referenced in our [Stardust privacy claims](/resources/guides/stardust-privacy-claims-debunked/) guide. The marketing implies one thing. The architecture does another.

Stardust is also account-based. You sign up, your cycle data is linked to that account, and it lives on company infrastructure. Pricing runs about $29.99/year.

## How Clue stores data

Clue is operated by Biowink GmbH out of Berlin. It has been on the market longer than Stardust. Its business model is subscription-based with no advertising. The company has research partnerships with Oxford and Stanford that feed into cycle science and product predictions.

Clue stores cycle data on cloud servers in the EU. That keeps the data under GDPR, which limits how Clue can use, share, or transfer user data commercially. There is no documented FTC enforcement history and no significant privacy settlement on record. Clue Plus is about $39.99/year, with a free tier that has no ads.

GDPR is a stronger commercial framework than what US-based apps operate under by default. It is still a policy framework, not an architectural one. The data is still on Clue's servers.

## Why neither is subpoena-proof

The structural issue for both apps is the same. If a company holds a copy of your cycle data on its servers, a court with valid jurisdiction can [subpoena](/resources/guides/can-police-access-period-tracker-data/) that data. In states with abortion restrictions, prosecutors have sought digital evidence including period tracking records. GDPR does not block compelled disclosure under valid legal process, especially when the request crosses jurisdictions.

Stardust's marketing framing does not change this. If the company can decrypt the data, the data can be produced. Clue's stronger commercial privacy posture does not change this either. If the data is on a server, it is reachable through legal process.

The only architecture that removes this exposure is on-device-only storage, where the company never creates a server copy in the first place. Floriva, Drip, and Euki use this model. Stardust and Clue do not.

## Pricing snapshot

Stardust: $29.99/year. Clue Plus: $39.99/year (free tier with no ads). Floriva: paid app with no free tier, with on-device storage for every user and no account required.

If annual cost is the deciding factor, Stardust is cheaper. If a verifiable privacy track record is the deciding factor, Clue is cleaner. If your cycle data should never exist on a company server, neither Stardust nor Clue is the answer.
