Stardust became one of the most downloaded apps in the App Store in the days after the Dobbs decision in June 2022, driven by its positioning as a privacy-first period tracker at exactly the moment reproductive privacy concerns peaked.

The scrutiny that followed was instructive.

## What Happened With Stardust Post-Dobbs

Within days of Stardust's surge, tech press (including Joseph Cox at Vice/Motherboard) and independent privacy researchers analyzed the app's data practices. Their finding: Stardust contained third-party SDK integrations, including AppsFlyer, an advertising attribution tracker, that were inconsistent with its privacy-first marketing. The app also required a phone number for registration, linking cycle data to a more identifiable credential than an email address.

Stardust subsequently updated its app, removed some trackers, and revised its privacy policy. CEO Rachel Moranis addressed the criticism and committed to improvements.

**The lesson:** Privacy marketing is responsive to scrutiny. Apps update their practices when attention is focused on them. The more durable question is whether an app's architecture creates meaningful privacy, independent of what the current marketing says.

## Current Status (Verify Before Relying)

Since Stardust has updated its app multiple times, any specific claims about current SDK presence should be independently verified:

1. **Exodus Privacy** (exodus-privacy.eu.org): Search for Stardust; check the date of the most recent analysis against the current app version
2. **App Store Privacy Label**: Check the "Data Linked to You" and "Data Used to Track You" sections in the App Store listing
3. **Privacy Policy**: Check for named analytics and advertising partners

If Stardust's current version contains no advertising attribution SDKs and its privacy policy has explicit, specific commitments, it may be a reasonable option for users who want a social-feature-rich app.

## The Phone Number Problem

Stardust requires a phone number for registration. This is a more identity-linked credential than email. Email accounts can be pseudonymous; phone numbers are tied to real identity in most cases through carrier records. A subpoena for Stardust records about a specific phone number is more directly actionable than a subpoena for records about an email address that may be pseudonymous.

## What "Privacy" Means in Each Case

**Stardust's privacy approach:** Policy-based. Commitments in the privacy policy, updates in response to scrutiny, stated intent not to sell or misuse data. Subject to change with ownership, financial pressure, or insufficient enforcement.

**Floriva's privacy approach:** Architectural. No readable server records means there is less company-held data to produce after legal demands, ownership changes, or policy revisions. This is not because Floriva would not comply with a valid legal demand. It is because there is less company-held data to produce.

## Who Each Is Right For

**Stardust is better if:**
- You want social features and a community
- You want astrology-integrated cycle content
- You are comfortable with cloud storage and account-based apps
- You are not in a legally sensitive reproductive health situation

**Floriva is better if:**
- Architectural privacy matters to you (no readable central records, not policy-based)
- You want local-first cycle and fertility notes
- You do not want your phone number linked to your health data
- Legal risk from reproductive health data is a concern

## What This Means for Floriva Users

The Stardust case is a useful benchmark for evaluating any "privacy-focused" period app: verify claims against architecture, check current SDK presence rather than trusting marketing statements, and understand that policy-based privacy is responsive to pressure while architecture-based privacy is not dependent on it.
