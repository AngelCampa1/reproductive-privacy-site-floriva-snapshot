## Why teen safety in period trackers is different

A teenager downloading a period tracker faces risks that adult users may not consider. Minors have less control over their devices, less understanding of data privacy, and less legal autonomy if their health data is accessed by authorities or other adults.

Most popular period trackers (Flo, Clue, Glow) are built for adult users. They require accounts (which means email addresses and identity data for minors), display ads (which means ad SDKs collecting data about a minor's reproductive health), and store data on servers (which means a third party holds a child's menstrual data).

The apps in this comparison take a different approach. None requires an account. None shows ads. All store data locally. The question is which one best serves a teenage user's specific needs.

## The threat model for teens

Teen period tracker users face a layered threat model.

**Digital threats**: Ad SDKs collecting reproductive health data from minors. Server-stored data accessible via subpoena. Data breaches exposing a minor's health information. COPPA violations when apps collect data from users under 13.

**Physical threats**: Parents, partners, or others with physical access to the device seeing tracking data. In some family situations, period tracking itself is something a teen needs to keep private.

**Educational gaps**: Many teens start tracking their period without understanding their cycle. An app that provides accurate, age-appropriate information serves them better than one that only offers empty logging fields.

The three apps compared here each address different parts of this threat model.

## Floriva: structurally private

Floriva's privacy architecture is the same for teens as for adults: no account for core tracking, no ads, and local-first core records. There is no sync, so nothing is transmitted. Website, billing, support, and store records are separate from core cycle tracking.

This reduces digital threats by default. There is no ad-SDK handling a minor's reproductive health events. There is no readable central cycle database for a parent, school, or law enforcement agency to request from Floriva. There is no account required for core tracking.

Floriva does not include sexual health education or puberty content. It is a tracking tool, not a health education platform. For teens who need cycle tracking without any content that might create conflict with parents or guardians, this is an advantage.

The limitation: Floriva is a paid app with no free tier. Most teens need a parent's payment method. The subscription name appears in app store purchase history. For some teens, this is fine. For others, it is a barrier.

## Euki: physical privacy and health education

Euki was built by the International Planned Parenthood Federation specifically for younger users and people in situations where privacy extends beyond the digital. Its decoy screen feature addresses a reality that purely technical solutions miss: someone may physically take your phone and demand to see what is on it.

The decoy screen shows a fake interface when activated. Your actual tracking data is hidden behind a separate access point. For teens in controlling family environments, abusive relationships, or encounters with authority figures, this feature provides cover that encryption alone cannot.

Euki also includes comprehensive sexual and reproductive health content for younger users. Topics include contraception, consent, STI information, and menstrual health. For teens whose health education is limited by their school or family environment, this content fills real gaps.

The content itself may concern some parents. The app provides frank, medically accurate information about sex and contraception. Whether this is a pro or con depends entirely on the family's values and the teen's needs.

Euki is free. No payment method required. No purchase history trail.

## Spot On: education-first tracking

Spot On was built by Planned Parenthood Federation of America. Like Euki, it pairs period tracking with reproductive health education. The content is age-appropriate and medically accurate, covering contraception methods, cycle biology, and sexual health basics.

Spot On includes contraception tracking, letting users log their birth control method alongside their cycle. For sexually active teens using contraception, this integration is practical.

The app stores data locally and requires no account. There are no ads, and the privacy architecture is sound.

The Planned Parenthood name on the app may be an issue in some communities and family contexts. The organization is politically polarized in the United States. A teen whose parent opposes Planned Parenthood may not want the app visible on their phone. Unlike Euki, Spot On does not offer a decoy screen.

Spot On is free with no in-app purchases.

## What mainstream apps get wrong for teens

Flo's free tier is ad-supported. Flo has a documented history of sharing reproductive health data with Facebook and Google before the FTC intervened. For teens, that history matters because a minor's cycle information deserves stronger protection than policy promises and post-settlement controls.

Clue requires an account with an email address. A minor creating an account ties their identity to their menstrual data on Clue's servers. Clue has the cleanest enforcement record among major trackers, but the architectural exposure remains.

Glow's community forums exposed users to inappropriate content, and the 2016 data exposure affected all users regardless of age. An app with social features and server-stored data presents elevated risks for minors.

None of these mainstream apps were designed with teen safety as a primary concern. They were designed for adults and used by teens because teenagers get periods too.

## Choosing for a teen

**If data privacy is the primary concern**: Floriva. No data leaves the device. The architecture makes privacy the default, not a setting. Requires parental payment method.

**If physical privacy matters**: Euki. The decoy screen addresses threats from people with physical device access. Free, no payment trail.

**If health education is the priority**: Euki or Spot On. Both provide age-appropriate reproductive health content. Spot On focuses specifically on contraception education.

**If the teen wants to choose**: Give them this comparison. Teens old enough to track their period are old enough to understand the trade-offs between these three options. All three are safe. The right choice depends on their specific situation.

## A note on parental involvement

Some parents want to monitor their teen's period tracking. None of these apps supports parental monitoring, and that is by design. A period tracker that reports to parents is a surveillance tool, not a health tool. Teens need private health spaces, and menstrual tracking is one of the most basic.

If a parent wants to support their teen's cycle tracking, the right approach is conversation, not monitoring. Help them choose an app that respects their privacy, understand that their health data belongs to them, and make yourself available if they have questions their app cannot answer.
