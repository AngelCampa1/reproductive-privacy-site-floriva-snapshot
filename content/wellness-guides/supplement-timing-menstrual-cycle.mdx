Supplement recommendations for the menstrual cycle range from evidence-based (iron for documented deficiency) to speculative (elaborate phase-by-phase supplement rotations with no controlled trial support). This guide separates the supplements with meaningful evidence from the ones where the marketing has outpaced the science.

*This article is for educational purposes and is not medical advice. Consult a healthcare provider before starting any supplement, especially iron. Test iron levels before supplementing. Excess iron is harmful.*

## Iron: The Strongest Case

**The evidence:** Menstruation is the primary cause of iron deficiency in people who menstruate. Blood loss depletes iron stores (ferritin) over time. Iron deficiency, even without anemia, produces fatigue, impaired exercise performance, reduced concentration, and cold intolerance.

**Who needs it:** Anyone with ferritin below 30 ng/mL should discuss supplementation with a provider. Some practitioners use a threshold of 50 ng/mL for optimal function. A ferritin test, not just hemoglobin, is necessary to assess iron stores.

**Timing:** Standard treatment is daily supplementation. For those who experience GI side effects from daily iron, alternate-day dosing has shown comparable absorption in some research. Timing iron doses during and after menstruation, when loss is active, is physiologically rational for intermittent supplementation strategies.

**Absorption optimization:** Take iron with vitamin C (50-100mg). Avoid taking it with calcium supplements, dairy, coffee, or tea, which inhibit absorption. Iron bisglycinate tends to produce fewer GI side effects than ferrous sulfate.

**Caution:** Do not supplement iron without testing. Excess iron accumulates and causes organ damage. Iron overload conditions (like hereditary hemochromatosis) are more common than many people realize.

## Magnesium: Good Evidence, Variable Quality

**The evidence:** Magnesium is involved in muscle contraction and relaxation, neurotransmitter function, and prostaglandin metabolism. Some studies suggest magnesium supplementation reduces menstrual cramp severity and may improve PMS symptoms (mood, bloating, sleep). The evidence is promising but limited by small trials and inconsistent methodologies.

**Forms matter:** Magnesium glycinate is well-absorbed and less likely to cause GI distress. Magnesium citrate has good bioavailability but can cause loose stools at higher doses. Magnesium oxide is cheap but poorly absorbed.

**Timing:** Magnesium can be taken daily. For targeted PMS and cramp relief, some practitioners recommend starting or increasing the dose in the luteal phase (after ovulation). The calming and muscle-relaxing effects may be most noticeable when taken in the evening.

**Dosing:** Typical supplemental doses range from 200-400mg of elemental magnesium daily. The tolerable upper intake level for supplemental magnesium is 350mg/day for adults, though some practitioners recommend higher doses with monitoring.

## Vitamin B6: Limited But Present Evidence

**The evidence:** Several small studies and a Cochrane review have examined B6 for PMS symptoms. There is modest evidence that B6 at doses of 50-100mg/day may reduce PMS-related mood symptoms, though study quality is generally low.

**Mechanism:** B6 is a cofactor in serotonin and dopamine synthesis. The theoretical basis for PMS benefit is that it supports neurotransmitter production during the luteal phase when serotonin signaling declines.

**Caution:** B6 at doses above 200mg/day over prolonged periods can cause peripheral neuropathy (nerve damage). Stay within recommended dosing ranges. The active form (pyridoxal-5-phosphate or P5P) may be more effective for individuals with impaired B6 conversion.

## Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism

**The evidence:** Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) compete with arachidonic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) for the cyclooxygenase enzymes that produce prostaglandins. Higher omega-3 intake shifts prostaglandin production away from the highly inflammatory Series 2 prostaglandins (which drive menstrual cramps) toward less inflammatory Series 3 prostaglandins.

Several trials show reduced menstrual pain with omega-3 supplementation, though the effect size varies. Doses in studies typically range from 1,000-2,000mg of combined EPA/DHA daily.

**Timing:** Consistent daily supplementation. The anti-inflammatory effect requires sustained intake over weeks to shift the omega-3/omega-6 balance in cell membranes. Starting only during menstruation is insufficient.

## What Lacks Evidence

**Seed cycling.** The practice of eating specific seeds in different cycle phases to modulate hormones. The proposed mechanism (phytoestrogens in flax and sesame, zinc and selenium in pumpkin and sunflower seeds) is theoretically plausible but has no controlled trial evidence demonstrating hormonal effects at dietary doses.

**Vitex (chasteberry).** Some small studies suggest vitex may improve PMS symptoms and regulate cycles. Evidence quality is low, mechanisms are not fully established, and it can interact with hormonal medications. Discuss with a provider before use.

**DIM (diindolylmethane).** Promoted for estrogen metabolism. While DIM is a metabolite of cruciferous vegetables and does interact with estrogen metabolism pathways, clinical evidence for supplemental DIM in menstrual health is sparse.

## What to Track

**Symptom logging with supplement start date.** Record your symptoms daily for at least two cycles before starting a supplement, then continue tracking after. This gives you a genuine before/after comparison rather than placebo-influenced perception.

**Iron levels before and after.** If supplementing iron, retest ferritin after 3 months to confirm improvement and adjust dosing.

**Side effects.** GI symptoms from iron, loose stools from magnesium, or any neurological symptoms from B6 should be noted and discussed with a provider.

## Common Mistakes

**Supplementing without testing.** Iron supplementation without a ferritin test is the most common and most dangerous mistake. Magnesium and B6 are generally safe at recommended doses, but even these should be taken with purpose, not just because a cycle-syncing chart recommended them.

**Expecting supplement-level effects from food doses.** The therapeutic dose of magnesium for cramps (200-400mg) requires supplementation for most people. Dietary sources alone rarely reach this level. Conversely, iron from diet (especially heme iron from meat) is well-absorbed and may be sufficient for mild deficiency.

**Rotating supplements by phase without evidence.** Complex phase-by-phase supplement protocols are not supported by research. Simple, consistent supplementation of nutrients you are actually deficient in produces better outcomes than elaborate rotation schedules.

## When to Get Help

Consult a provider if: you suspect iron deficiency (test first, then supplement), PMS or menstrual pain is not improving with conservative measures, you want to start multiple supplements and are unsure about interactions, or your symptoms suggest an underlying condition (PCOS, endometriosis, PMDD) that supplements cannot treat.
