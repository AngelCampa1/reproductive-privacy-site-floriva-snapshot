Not every period tracker works for every person. The app that works for a 16-year-old tracking her first cycles is wrong for someone using fertility awareness as birth control, and both are wrong for someone whose main concern is keeping cycle data away from law enforcement.

This assessment walks you through the questions that actually separate one tracker from another.

## Question 1: What's Your Primary Reason for Tracking?

**Why are you logging your cycle?**

- **Option A: Basic period prediction.** You want to know when your period is coming and that's about it. You need a simple calendar, not a feature-heavy platform.
- **Option B: Fertility awareness or TTC.** You're tracking ovulation windows, basal body temperature, or cervical mucus. You need an app with fertility-specific inputs and algorithms.
- **Option C: Symptom management.** You're tracking pain, mood, energy, or symptoms related to conditions like PCOS or endometriosis. You need flexible custom logging.
- **Option D: Privacy above all else.** Your main goal is keeping a record that nobody else can access. Features matter less than architecture.

If you picked D, skip ahead to Question 3. Storage model is your deciding factor.

## Question 2: How Many Data Points Do You Want to Log?

**What does your ideal daily entry look like?**

- **Option A: Period start and end dates only.** Minimal input, minimal complexity.
- **Option B: Period dates plus a few symptoms.** Flow intensity, mood, maybe cramps.
- **Option C: Comprehensive daily logging.** Temperature, cervical mucus, medications, sleep, exercise, custom tags.

Option A users should avoid apps that push you into daily engagement with dozens of fields. Option C users should avoid apps that limit custom symptom categories.

## Question 3: Where Should Your Data Live?

**This is the most important question in this assessment.**

- **Option A: On the company's servers is fine.** You trust the app company and aren't concerned about subpoenas or breaches. Most mainstream apps (Flo, Clue, Ovia) work this way.
- **Option B: On my device only.** You want your data stored locally, with no account creation and no server upload. Apps like Floriva and Drip use this model.
- **Option C: Encrypted cloud sync.** You want data accessible across devices but encrypted so the company can't read it. This is the rarest architecture.

If you live in a state with abortion restrictions, Option B or C removes a category of legal risk that Option A does not. See [privacy by state](/period-tracker-privacy) for specifics.

## Question 4: Does Your State Matter?

**Do you live in or travel to a state with restrictive reproductive health laws?**

- **Option A: Yes.** Your tracker's data storage model is a legal question, not just a preference. Server-stored data can be subpoenaed. On-device data requires physical access to your phone.
- **Option B: No, but I might move.** Consider choosing an architecture that protects you regardless of jurisdiction.
- **Option C: I'm outside the US.** GDPR and similar frameworks provide baseline protections, but server-stored data is still server-stored data.

Option A makes Question 3 non-negotiable: you need on-device or end-to-end encrypted storage.

## Question 5: What's Your Budget?

**What are you willing to pay for period tracking?**

- **Option A: Nothing.** Free apps either show ads, limit features, or monetize your data in ways that aren't always transparent.
- **Option B: Under $5/month.** Most premium tracker subscriptions fall here. Check what the free tier excludes before committing.
- **Option C: One-time purchase preferred.** You'd rather pay once than subscribe. Fewer options exist here, but they do exist.

Free isn't always cheaper when the cost is your data. Check the business model for any free app you're considering.

## Question 6: What Platform Are You On?

**Where do you need the app to work?**

- **Option A: iPhone only.**
- **Option B: Android only.**
- **Option C: Both, or I switch between them.**

Cross-platform availability varies. Some privacy-focused apps are iOS-only or Android-only. If you need both, your options narrow.

## Your Results

**Tally your answers and find your pattern:**

**Mostly A answers across all questions:** You're a casual tracker with low privacy concern. Most mainstream apps will work, but read the privacy policy anyway. Flo and Clue are full-featured but cloud-stored.

**Option D on Question 1 or Option A on Question 4:** Privacy is your main filter. Start with [the best private period tracker list](/resources/best/best-private-period-tracker-apps) and evaluate only apps with on-device storage. Floriva fits this profile.

**Option B or C on Question 2, Option B on Question 5:** You want comprehensive tracking and you're willing to pay for it. Compare feature sets across premium tiers, but verify the storage model first.

**Option B on Question 3, any answer on Question 4:** You've made your storage decision. Filter for [zero-knowledge or on-device apps](/resources/guides/what-is-zero-knowledge-period-tracker) and evaluate features within that set.

**Option C on Question 1, Option C on Question 2:** Symptom management is your priority. Look for apps with custom fields, exportable reports for doctor visits, and no features locked behind paywalls.

The right tracker is the one that matches your threat model, your tracking needs, and your budget, in that order. Features you can add later. Data architecture you cannot change after a year of logging.
