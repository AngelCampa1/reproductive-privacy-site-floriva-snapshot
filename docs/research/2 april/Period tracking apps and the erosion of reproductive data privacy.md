# Period tracking apps and the erosion of reproductive data privacy

**Every major period tracking app investigated between 2020 and 2025 was found to share, sell, or expose sensitive reproductive health data — often in direct contradiction of privacy promises made to users.** Federal regulators brought enforcement actions against Flo Health and Premom, a federal jury found Meta liable for intercepting menstrual data, and data brokers sold location information that could identify visitors to abortion clinics. The cumulative record reveals a systemic failure across the femtech industry and the location-data supply chain, one that intensified after the Supreme Court's 2022 *Dobbs* decision eliminated federal abortion protections and made reproductive surveillance a concrete legal threat.

This report documents every verified incident from 2020–2025, drawn from FTC filings, court dockets, regulatory decisions, and investigative journalism.

## The FTC's landmark case against Flo Health established new enforcement precedent

On **January 13, 2021**, the Federal Trade Commission announced a complaint against Flo Health, Inc. (FTC File No. **192 3133**, Docket **C-4747**), the developer of the Flo Period & Ovulation Tracker — the most downloaded health and fitness app in Apple's App Store in 2019, with over **100 million downloads** and **16 million U.S. users**. The complaint alleged seven counts of violating Section 5(a) of the FTC Act through deceptive practices related to data sharing.

Beginning in **June 2016**, Flo embedded software development kits from Facebook, Flurry, Google (Fabric and Analytics), and AppsFlyer that transmitted "Custom App Events" with descriptive titles such as `R_PREGNANCY_WEEK_CHOSEN` and `P_ACCEPT_PUSHES_PERIOD` — paired with unique advertising identifiers linkable to individual devices. Flo's privacy policy during this period explicitly promised that shared data "exclud[ed] information regarding your marked cycles, pregnancy, symptoms, notes and other information." The FTC found this representation flatly false. Data transmitted included **pregnancy status, menstrual period dates, intention to become pregnant, and reproductive health symptoms**. The recipients — Facebook, Google, AppsFlyer, and Flurry — were not contractually restricted from using the data for their own purposes, and Facebook used Flo's event data for internal R&D.

The scheme was exposed by a **Wall Street Journal** investigation published **February 22, 2019**, which demonstrated that reporters could intercept unencrypted identifying health information flowing from Flo to Facebook. Flo cut off Facebook data sharing the next day and received over **300 user complaints** expressing outrage.

The consent order, finalized **June 22, 2021** by a 4-0-1 vote (Chair Lina Khan recused), imposed no monetary penalty but broke new ground in several respects. It required Flo to **notify affected users directly** — the first time the FTC ordered a company to inform users of a privacy enforcement action. Flo was also required to instruct all third-party recipients to **destroy the shared data**, obtain **affirmative express consent** before any future health data sharing, and submit to an **independent privacy review** within 180 days. Commissioners Rohit Chopra and Rebecca Kelly Slaughter concurred in part but dissented from the failure to charge Flo under the Health Breach Notification Rule, arguing the statute's "plain language" covered the conduct. Their dissent catalyzed subsequent HBNR enforcement against GoodRx and BetterHelp in 2023.

## A $59.5 million class action and a jury verdict against Meta followed

The FTC's administrative action was shadowed by private litigation that ultimately proved far more consequential. On **January 29, 2021** — sixteen days after the FTC announcement — eight named plaintiffs led by Erica Frasco filed *Frasco et al. v. Flo Health, Inc., et al.* (Case No. **3:21-cv-00757-JD**) in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California before Judge James Donato. The class encompassed all U.S. Flo App users who entered menstruation or pregnancy data between **November 1, 2016 and February 28, 2019**, with a California subclass asserting claims under the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA § 632) and the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA).

The case named Flo Health, Meta Platforms, Google, Flurry, and AppsFlyer as defendants. AppsFlyer was voluntarily dismissed in 2022. The remaining defendants settled in sequence: **Flurry for $3.5 million** (March 2025), **Google for $48 million** (July 3, 2025), and **Flo Health for $8 million** (July 31, 2025 — the day before the jury returned its verdict). The combined **$59.5 million settlement** covers the nationwide class, with California residents receiving **twice the pro rata share** of non-California members. The settlement administrator is A.B. Data, Ltd., and the official claims website is periodtrackerdataprivacylitigation.com.

Meta alone proceeded to trial. On **August 1, 2025**, after approximately three hours of deliberation, a federal jury unanimously found Meta **liable under CIPA § 632** for intentionally eavesdropping on private health communications through its SDK embedded in Flo. The jury concluded that users had a reasonable expectation of privacy and that Meta had not obtained consent. Under CIPA § 637.2, Meta faces potential statutory damages of **$5,000 per violation or three times actual damages** — potentially billions given the class size. Judge Donato denied all of Meta's post-trial motions on September 15, 2025. As of April 2026, the $59.5 million settlement awaits preliminary approval at a hearing scheduled for **April 16, 2026**, and Meta's appeal remains pending.

## Clue's GDPR fortress has cracks the public pledges don't acknowledge

Clue, developed by **BioWink GmbH** in Berlin, markets its European jurisdiction as a definitive privacy advantage, emphasizing that it operates under "the strictest privacy laws in the world." The app has been downloaded over **100 million times** and serves roughly **10–11 million monthly users**. After *Dobbs*, Co-CEOs Carrie Walter and Audrey Tsang issued an unequivocal pledge: "We promise you that we will never turn your private health data over to any authority that could use it against you." In November 2024, CEO Rhiannon White went further in a viral TikTok: "If we are ever subpoenaed, we will not comply."

Independent investigations have identified several tensions between these pledges and actual practice. The **Norwegian Consumer Council's** January 2020 "Out of Control" report found that 10 popular apps analyzed — including Clue — collectively fed personal information to **at least 135 third-party advertising and profiling companies**, sharing Android Advertising IDs, IP addresses, device identifiers, and demographic data. **Privacy International's** December 2020 Data Subject Access Request investigation found that every interaction with the Clue app resulted in data stored on Clue's servers linked to user ID, device ID, and location precise enough to identify the researcher's borough. Privacy International also noted that Clue **initially failed to provide a complete list of third parties** receiving user data, calling it "an oversight" in a follow-up.

Most damaging was a **Vice/Motherboard** report in May 2022 revealing that reporters purchased data from the data broker **Narrative** for approximately **$100** that identified Android devices with Clue installed. While the purchased data consisted of advertising IDs rather than in-app health data, it demonstrated that Clue user identities were circulating in the commercial data marketplace. Narrative subsequently removed all pregnancy and menstruation app install data from its platform.

Clue's own privacy policy reveals additional caveats. Despite claiming never to share tracked health data with advertisers, the policy states that Clue collects and shares "a minimal amount of technical data with advertising networks" including device identifiers and **date of birth** — on an **opt-out, not opt-in**, basis. The policy also acknowledges that data may be shared "for legal requirements — when required by law, legal process, or government request" and that "the risk of such disclosure... cannot be eliminated." **Mozilla's** August 2022 *Privacy Not Included review gave Clue a **warning label**, citing extensive data collection, opt-out advertising data sharing, and a failure to meet minimum security standards (the app accepted **single-character passwords** like "1"). No GDPR enforcement action has been filed against BioWink, and no independent third-party privacy certification is publicly documented for the company — unlike Flo, which obtained dual ISO 27001/27701 certifications post-FTC settlement.

## Stardust's privacy pledge collapsed within 72 hours of scrutiny

The Stardust period tracking app, an astrology-themed tracker founded by CEO Rachel Moranis, became the most consequential case study in post-*Dobbs* privacy theater. On **June 24, 2022** — the day the Supreme Court issued *Dobbs* — Moranis posted a TikTok announcing Stardust was "the first period tracker to implement end-to-end encryption." Downloads exploded: from roughly **3,000 installs daily** to **200,000 on June 25**, a **4,400% surge** that sent Stardust to the **#1 position** on the U.S. App Store, surpassing TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube.

Within 72 hours, three independent investigations dismantled the claims:

- **TechCrunch** (Sarah Perez and security editor Zack Whittaker, June 27) ran a network traffic analysis and found that Stardust shared users' **phone numbers with Mixpanel**, a third-party analytics service, when users logged in via phone number. When pressed on the encryption implementation, Moranis described standard **SSL in transit and AES-256 at rest** — not end-to-end encryption. After TechCrunch's inquiry, Stardust **silently removed all references to "end-to-end encryption" from its privacy policy**. On June 30, Whittaker analyzed the updated app and found that locally-generated encryption keys were being **uploaded to Stardust's own servers**, meaning the company could decrypt all user data.

- **Vice/Motherboard** (Samantha Cole, June 27) discovered that Stardust's privacy policy contained the clause: data may be disclosed "whether or not legally required" — meaning Stardust volunteered to cooperate with law enforcement even absent legal compulsion. After Motherboard requested comment at approximately 4:00 PM EST, **Stardust changed the policy within hours**, replacing "whether or not legally required" with "when legally required."

- **Privacy International** subsequently identified **at least six third-party services** processing user data — Rownd, Firebase, Mixpanel, OneSignal, RevenueCat, and Cloudflare — that were **not disclosed in the privacy policy**. The only third party actually named in the policy was AppsFlyer.

No FTC enforcement action has been taken against Stardust. However, attorneys affiliated with Milberg LLC are investigating the company for potential Federal Wiretap Act violations. Stardust remains operational with over one million users, no longer claims end-to-end encryption, and has partnered with authentication platform Rownd to separate identity data from health data — though all data remains server-side and theoretically accessible via legal process.

## Data brokers built a surveillance infrastructure around reproductive health clinics

The period-app data pipeline was only one vector. A parallel market in **geolocation data** from smartphones enabled tracking of visits to abortion clinics at industrial scale, with documented sales to anti-abortion organizations, federal law enforcement, and defense contractors.

**SafeGraph** was the first broker exposed. On **May 3, 2022**, Vice/Motherboard reporter Joseph Cox purchased a week's worth of aggregated location data covering **600+ Planned Parenthood locations** for approximately **$160** through SafeGraph's self-serve online shop. The data showed where visitors came from (by census block), visit duration, and subsequent destinations. Some entries tracked as few as **4–5 devices**, making de-anonymization feasible. The same day, Motherboard reported that the **CDC had purchased $420,000** in SafeGraph data covering April 2021–April 2022 for COVID-related and non-COVID purposes. SafeGraph removed family planning center data the next day; Senator Elizabeth Warren and 13 Senate Democrats demanded answers, and SafeGraph eventually shuttered its entire self-serve data shop.

**Near Intelligence** went further. A **Wall Street Journal** investigation (May 2023) revealed that the Veritas Society — a fund established by Wisconsin Right to Life — used Near's geolocation data to geofence Planned Parenthood clinics and parking lots across **48 states**, serving **14.3 million targeted anti-abortion ads** in 2020 alone to clinic visitors' Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat feeds. Senator Ron Wyden's investigation uncovered that Near's Chief Privacy Officer confirmed the company had **no technical controls** to prevent targeting people at sensitive locations until summer 2022, and that Near had been selling location data to defense contractor **AELIUS Exploitation Technologies** for three years, which resold it to the **Defense Department and U.S. intelligence agencies** — despite Near's website stating it did not sell data to governments. Near Intelligence filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in late 2023, with former executives confirmed to be under criminal investigation.

The FTC responded with an escalating series of enforcement actions against data brokers:

- **Kochava** (August 2022): Sued for selling geolocation data from **61 million+ devices** weekly that could trace individuals from reproductive health clinics to single-family residences. Case pending.
- **X-Mode Social/Outlogic** (January 2024): First-ever FTC settlement with a data broker over sensitive location data. X-Mode sold raw data enabling tracking of visits to reproductive health clinics and created custom audience segments based on healthcare visits. Banned from selling sensitive location data.
- **Gravy Analytics/Venntel** (December 2024): Venntel, a Gravy Analytics subsidiary, sold location data to **DHS, DEA, FBI, and IRS**, and supplied data to Babel Street's Locate X and Fog Data Science. Banned from selling sensitive location data; required to delete all historical data.
- **Mobilewalla** (December 2024): First-ever FTC prohibition on collecting consumer data from real-time bidding exchanges for non-advertising purposes.

Perhaps the most alarming demonstration came from **Babel Street's Locate X tool**. In October 2024, Atlas Privacy — using access obtained merely by claiming to be "contemplating government contract work" — used Locate X to **track a device from a residence in Alabama** (where abortion is banned) to an **abortion clinic in Tallahassee, Florida**, following the device along a highway, past a Lowe's, to a church, and to the clinic for a two-hour visit, then back to Alabama. Over **700 unique devices** were identified at a single Florida abortion clinic over three years.

## Premom, Glow, Maya, and other apps complete the pattern

The Flo and Stardust cases were not outliers. Multiple other reproductive health apps were found sharing data:

**Premom (Easy Healthcare Corporation)** faced the FTC's second-ever Health Breach Notification Rule enforcement action (Case **1:23-cv-03107**, N.D. Illinois, filed May 17, 2023). Premom's ovulation tracker shared menstrual cycle and fertility data with Google and AppsFlyer via descriptive custom events, and — more unusually — shared device identifiers and precise geolocation with two Chinese companies, **Umeng (Alibaba)** and **Jiguang/Aurora Mobile**, from 2018 through 2020. The settlement imposed a **$100,000 federal civil penalty** plus **$100,000 to state attorneys general** in Connecticut, Oregon, and the District of Columbia, permanently banned health data sharing for advertising, and required direct user notification.

**Glow, Inc.** (maker of Glow and Eve by Glow) settled with the **California Attorney General in September 2020** for **$250,000** after investigators found that the app's "Partner Connect" feature automatically granted data access to anyone who sent a linking request — without the recipient's confirmation — exposing intimate health data including miscarriage and abortion history. Consumer Reports had originally discovered the vulnerability in July 2016. In **October 2024**, security researcher Ovi Liber discovered a separate API bug exposing personal data of approximately **25 million Glow users**, including names, age groups, location data, and user-uploaded images.

**Privacy International's** September 2019 "No Body's Business But Mine" report examined six period trackers and found that **Maya** (by India-based Plackal Tech, 5+ million downloads) shared contraception choices, period dates, mood data, sexual activity, and diary entries with Facebook **before the user even agreed to the privacy policy**. **MIA Fem** (by Mobapp Development) similarly transmitted menstruation cycle data and article recommendations based on sexual habits to both Facebook and AppsFlyer. Following the report, Plackal Tech removed both Facebook SDKs from Maya.

**Ovia Health** drew scrutiny from a **Washington Post** investigation (April 2019) revealing that employers who purchased Ovia's corporate wellness program could access **aggregated employee reproductive data** — average age, trimester distribution, high-risk pregnancy percentages, C-section rates, infertility rates, and return-to-work timing. The company's terms granted an "irrevocable license" to "utilize and exploit" personal data. Ovia was later acquired by Labcorp.

**Natural Cycles**, the FDA-cleared contraceptive app, faces a class action filed **December 4, 2024** (*S.A., et al. v. NaturalCycles USA Corp.*, Case No. 3:25-cv-10421-WHO, N.D. Cal.) alleging hidden tracking technologies from Mixpanel, AddShoppers, Google, and TikTok captured reproductive and sexual health data in real time — despite the app charging **$12.99/month** and marketing itself as privacy-protective. The litigation is ongoing.

## The regulatory landscape shifted but remains incomplete

The period between 2020 and 2025 saw the most significant regulatory response to reproductive data exploitation in U.S. history, driven largely by the *Dobbs* decision. **President Biden's Executive Order 14076** (July 8, 2022) directed the FTC to protect consumer privacy related to reproductive healthcare and HHS to strengthen HIPAA protections — though both orders were **revoked by President Trump in January 2025**. The FTC broadened its definition of "health information" to include "anything that conveys information — or enables an inference — about a consumer's health," explicitly encompassing location data showing visits to clinics.

Congress launched multiple investigations. The House Oversight Committee sent letters to five data brokers and five health app companies in July 2022. Senator Warren's Health and Location Data Protection Act would ban data brokers from selling location and health data entirely, though it has not passed. At the state level, Washington's My Health My Data Act and Nevada's Consumer Health Data Privacy Law created new obligations for apps handling reproductive data.

Mozilla's August 2022 *Privacy Not Included survey found that **18 of 25 reproductive health apps** received warning labels, while ORCHA's parallel analysis found **84% of 25 period trackers shared data with third parties**. Only a handful of apps — **Euki** (by Women Help Women), **Drip**, and **Periodical** — store data exclusively on-device and earned recommendations from independent reviewers.

## Conclusion

The documented record from 2020–2025 reveals not isolated lapses but a structural pattern: reproductive health apps systematically embedded third-party SDKs that siphoned intimate data to advertising and analytics platforms, while privacy policies obscured or contradicted these practices. The data broker ecosystem compounded the risk by enabling anyone — from anti-abortion activists to federal agencies — to track clinic visits for as little as $100–$160. Enforcement escalated from the FTC's no-fine Flo settlement in 2021 to six data broker actions by December 2024 and a landmark jury verdict against Meta in August 2025, but the regulatory framework remains fragmented. Biden-era executive orders have been revoked, proposed federal legislation has stalled, and the fundamental tension persists: apps that collect reproductive data server-side remain vulnerable to legal process regardless of corporate pledges. The only period trackers that have withstood scrutiny are those that never collect the data in the first place.
